On August 2nd, [the EU AI Act's enforcement era began](https://commission.europa.eu/news-and-media/news/safer-and-more-transparent-ai-2026-08-02_en). The European Commission's AI Office activated its full enforcement powers. The rules for high-risk AI systems — those used in employment decisions, credit assessments, education, and more — are now legally binding, not advisory. Fines reach €15 million or 3% of global annual turnover, whichever is larger.

Two days later, [Obsidian Security raised $85 million](https://siliconangle.com/2026/08/04/obsidian-security-raises-85m-ai-agents-create-cybersecuritys-next-major-attack-surface/) at a $1.1 billion unicorn valuation. Their business: giving enterprises visibility into what their AI agents are actually doing at runtime — which SaaS systems they're accessing, which permissions they're using, what actions they're taking.

The timing is not a coincidence. Both events are pointing at the same structural gap.

## What the Law Now Requires

The EU AI Act doesn't just require that AI systems perform well. It requires that someone can show they were supervised. The high-risk provisions that went live on August 2nd mandate risk management systems, human oversight mechanisms, and documentation that lets regulators evaluate whether those controls existed and functioned.

[The AI Office can now request documentation, run technical evaluations, demand compliance measures, restrict models from the EU market, and issue those fines](https://beam.ai/agentic-insights/eu-ai-act-enforcement-august-2-2026-gpai-fines). For any organization deploying AI agents in covered domains — hiring workflows, customer credit decisions, education management — that means maintaining an auditable record of what each agent did, under what conditions, and what human check existed on it.

An interactive voice agent must tell users it's AI. An AI system making decisions in a high-risk category must have human oversight documented in a form that can survive regulatory review. These requirements assume enterprises know where their AI agents are deployed, what data they're touching, and what they decided.

## What the Market Is Saying

That assumption is wrong for most enterprises, and the market knows it.

Obsidian's round follows a specific demand signal: [nearly 70% of its client base now allows AI agents to interact with business data](https://www.obsidiansecurity.com/news/unlocking-ai-potential-securely). Those agents have access to SaaS applications, document stores, CRM systems, and internal APIs. They're taking actions — reading, writing, executing — against enterprise data in real time.

The reason Obsidian exists is that most enterprises have no runtime visibility into any of that. They know the agent was deployed. They can see the output it produced. They cannot reconstruct what it touched, in what sequence, with what permissions, or whether it behaved consistently with what they thought they were authorizing. Over 100 customers now pay Obsidian more than $100,000 a year for that visibility. Fourteen pay more than $1 million.

Those aren't security budgets. Those are the price of not having an answer when a regulator asks: *what was your human oversight mechanism?*

## The Inventory Problem

The compliance problem starts earlier than oversight. You can't provide oversight of what you don't know you're running.

[IBM research from Think 2026](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-05-05-think-2026-ibm-delivers-the-blueprint-for-the-ai-operating-model-as-the-ai-divide-widens) found that large enterprises will average over 1,600 deployed AI agents by end of year. Seventy percent say their governance is not fit for purpose. Only 18% maintain a current inventory of what agents they're actually operating.

That means 82% of large enterprises cannot tell you, on a given day, which of their AI agents are running, in which jurisdictions, against which data, with which user populations. The EU AI Act's high-risk provisions require knowing all of those things. The enforcement architecture assumes organizations have that visibility. The IBM data says they mostly don't.

[Microsoft addressed this directly in August with Zero Trust for AI guidance](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/security/blog/2026/08/04/advance-zero-trust-for-ai-new-tools-and-guidance-to-secure-ai-agents-and-devsecops/) — operationalizing security controls for AI agents as a natural extension of the same frameworks enterprises already use for human identity governance. The analogy is correct: you wouldn't run a financial system where you couldn't audit which accounts were accessed and by whom. But that's functionally what most enterprises are doing with their agents today.

## What "Human Oversight" Actually Requires

The EU AI Act's human oversight requirement isn't satisfied by having a person who technically could review the agent. It requires that the oversight is meaningful — that a human can intervene, that decisions can be traced, and that the system flags anomalies to the person responsible.

That's an infrastructure question, not a policy one. You can write a human oversight policy in an afternoon. Building the instrumentation that makes it real — logging which agent took which action, against which data, with which outcome, and surfacing that in a form a human can review — takes actual infrastructure.

This is why the week of August 2nd through 4th is worth reading as a single story. The law went live, and the market immediately validated that the infrastructure to comply with it is in high demand and dramatically under-supplied. Obsidian's round is the signal that enterprises are beginning to treat agent visibility as a cost of operation, not an optional enhancement.

## The Verification Layer Is Now Compliance Infrastructure

For years, the argument for agent verification was about performance: you should know whether your agents are doing good work. That case was compelling but easy to defer. Evaluation infrastructure could wait until the deployment was stable.

It can't wait anymore. The audit trail that tells you whether an agent is performing its task correctly is the same audit trail that demonstrates to a regulator you had oversight of it. These are not two separate investments. They're the same instrumentation, serving two masters at once.

The organizations that built verification infrastructure before August 2nd have a head start. The ones that deferred it are now running a compliance gap and a performance gap simultaneously.

The EU AI Act didn't change what good agent governance looks like. It changed who's asking for proof of it.
