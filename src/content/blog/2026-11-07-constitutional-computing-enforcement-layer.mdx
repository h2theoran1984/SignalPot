[Kihan, a San Francisco startup, announced its formation this week](https://www.hpcwire.com/aiwire/2026/11/06/kihan-launches-constitutional-computing-to-bring-enforceable-authority-to-enterprise-ai-agents/) alongside a product concept called Constitutional Computing. The idea is simple and the name is unusually honest about the problem: AI agent governance currently relies on the model following instructions it received during training. Constitutional Computing replaces that with an independent authorization system outside the AI itself — one that checks, at the point of execution, whether an action is actually authorized before the agent takes it.

The launch is a 5-person team with no shipping product yet. Under normal circumstances, that would make it a press release, not a story. But the timing places it in an interesting context. By September 2026, [enterprise AI deployment spending had reached an estimated $176.8 billion](https://www.tokenpost.com/news/business/48305), up from roughly $33 billion a year earlier. That is a fivefold increase in twelve months. And this same week, [Capgemini's World Cloud Report for Financial Services](https://www.capgemini.com/news/press-releases/financial-institutions-accelerate-transition-to-multi-agent-ai-in-drive-for-bottom-line-value/) reported that only 5% of financial institutions deploying multi-agent AI have actually institutionalized it — meaning the other 95% can't demonstrate returns or reliably extend what their agents can do.

The industry spent $176 billion building the planes. Now it is trying to figure out the air traffic control.

## The Architecture Distinction Is Real

Constitutional Computing is not a new concept in the abstract. The problem of constraining AI agent behavior has been discussed since the early days of model deployment. What is new is how explicitly the framing rejects the existing approach.

The conventional guardrail lives inside the model — an instruction, a system prompt, a fine-tuned behavior — that tells the agent what it shouldn't do. The agent interprets this the same way it interprets everything else: probabilistically, within context, as one signal among many. The guardrail is enforceable only to the extent that the model's weights treat it as a constraint. Under distribution shift, adversarial prompts, or just sufficiently complex multi-step chains, that enforcement degrades.

Kihan's claim is that authorization needs to be independent of the model's own reasoning — a separate system that the agent cannot reason its way around because the agent never consults it. The agent proposes an action; the authorization layer evaluates it; the action either proceeds or doesn't. The model doesn't get to argue.

This is how ACLs work. It's how permission systems in operating systems work. It's how database row-level security works. The idea that it should work this way for AI agents is not conceptually bold. What's notable is that building this for agents deployed across heterogeneous enterprise environments — agents built on different models, using different frameworks, connecting to different tool ecosystems — is genuinely hard and hasn't been solved at scale.

Kihan is targeting agents built on models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google, running on frameworks like Hermes Agent, and using the Model Context Protocol (MCP) for tool access. That's a reasonable slice of enterprise deployment as it looks today. Whether the architecture holds in practice at that breadth is the question that will only be answered once there are actual enterprise deployments to test it against.

## What $176 Billion Worth of Deployment Teaches You

The spending curve is worth pausing on. [Enterprise AI deployment did not grow from $33 billion to $176 billion](https://www.tokenpost.com/news/business/48305) because enterprises solved the trust problem. It grew because enterprises were under competitive pressure to move faster than the trust problem was resolved.

The result is that the industry now has an enormous installed base of agents operating under governance architectures that the people who deployed them mostly didn't design. System prompts. Informal scope agreements between the agent vendor and the enterprise buyer. Policy documents that describe what the agent should do in a form that neither the agent nor any technical system can verify. Behavioral expectations that live in a Confluence page and are enforced by hope.

[59% of financial institutions are running multi-agent AI](https://www.manilatimes.net/2026/11/05/tmt-newswire/globenewswire/financial-institutions-accelerate-transition-to-multi-agent-ai-in-drive-for-bottom-line-value/) in some form. Only 10% have achieved the orchestration level required to operate them autonomously. The gap between those numbers is not a capability gap. It's a trust gap: organizations can deploy agents but can't extend their autonomy, because there's no mechanism for a human decision-maker to verify that the agent is operating within the scope it was authorized for.

That verification problem is the one that any Constitutional Computing architecture is trying to solve. It is also the same problem that shows up when [a bank writes new terms of service making customers liable for what their AI agents do](/blog/wells-fargo-agent-liability-authorization-gap), or when an enterprise discovers it can't demonstrate to a regulator what a production agent was authorized to do when a disputed transaction occurred.

## The Enforcement Layer Is Becoming Its Own Market

What Kihan's launch signals — regardless of whether Kihan itself succeeds — is that the enforcement layer for AI agents is now a recognized product category. A year ago, this problem was discussed as a future challenge. Now there is capital, teams, and product development aimed specifically at it.

[Snowflake is grounding agents in its Horizon Catalog](https://aiagentsdirectory.com/news/ai-agents-news-brief-november-5-2026) to control what data agents can access. [IBM's watsonx.governance platform](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/954548941/kihan-launches-constitutional-computing-to-bring-enforceable-authority-to-enterprise-ai-agents) is becoming an integration target for external enforcement layers. Wells Fargo has decided the enforcement problem is the customer's problem, which is a governance position in its own right.

These are not coincidences. They are the market responding to the same underlying reality: enterprises spent heavily on agent deployment, discovered that "deployed" and "controllable" are different properties, and are now allocating resources to close the gap.

The organizations that figure out enforcement before they are forced to by a failed audit, a regulatory inquiry, or a liability dispute are the ones who will be able to extend their agent deployments. The organizations that treat it as a future concern are building that future concern into every agent they add to the fleet today.

## What Practitioners Should Take Away

Constitutional Computing is not ready to drop into your stack. Kihan's first enterprise evaluations are targeted for December. But the architecture it describes — authorization verified independently of the model, at execution time, against a formal representation of what the agent was actually authorized to do — is the right description of the problem.

What you can do now: treat agent authorization as a documentation problem. Before an agent is granted access to a system, there should be a verifiable record of what it was authorized to do — not a screenshot of a system prompt, but a versioned, auditable scope definition that can serve as evidence when "what was this agent authorized to do" becomes a question with legal weight.

That record is what an enforcement layer needs to function. Building it now, before you need it, is cheaper than reconstructing it after something goes wrong.

The industry spent $176 billion on the deployment wave. The enforcement wave is just starting. The two are going to have to meet somewhere.
