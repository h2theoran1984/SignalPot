This week, [Experian announced a product called Agent Trust](https://www.experianplc.com/newsroom/press-releases/2026/experian-announces-agent-trust-to-power-trusted-ai-driven-commer) — a dedicated platform for credentialing, verifying, and credentializing AI agents that transact on behalf of consumers. Not monitoring them. Not logging them. Credentialing them — the same way Experian has spent fifty years credentialing the humans who apply for mortgages, open bank accounts, and make commercial decisions.

That's a category shift, not a product launch.

When a company whose entire business model is built on the premise that *identity and trustworthiness can be formally assessed* extends that premise to AI agents, it's telling you something about where the industry is headed. The question of "can you trust this agent?" has moved from philosophy to infrastructure.

## Why This Is Not Just Experian's Problem

The obvious reading is that Experian is protecting e-commerce and financial services from rogue agents — AI systems acting on behalf of consumers that overextend their authorization, make unauthorized purchases, or behave unpredictably in high-stakes transactions. That reading is correct. But the harder problem is broader.

[A new HBR analysis published this week](https://hbr.org/2026/09/to-adopt-ai-at-scale-employees-need-to-trust-agents) surfaced the numbers behind enterprise agent adoption anxiety. The headline stat: 74% of enterprises report they rely on testing to catch agent failures before they reach production. Only 19% have an automated release gate — a hard check that blocks deployment if an agent falls below a performance threshold. In other words, three-quarters of enterprises are running what amounts to a wish: "we tested it and it seemed fine."

That's not a process problem. It's a trust architecture problem. And it shows up everywhere agents are being deployed — not just in consumer commerce, but in enterprise workflows, internal tools, and cross-organizational automations. Organizations are deploying agents faster than they can establish any reliable basis for trusting what those agents will do.

Experian's answer is to apply credit bureau logic to this problem: give agents a verifiable identity, attach an authorization scope to that identity, and create a mechanism for counterparties to check both before an agent acts. For consumer transactions, that's a clean fit. For enterprise-to-enterprise agent interactions — where one organization's agent is handing off a task to another organization's agent, often through a protocol like A2A — the same logic applies. You need to know who this agent is, who sent it, and what it's been authorized to do. Right now, most organizations can't answer any of those questions for agents arriving from outside their own stack.

## The Infrastructure Is Being Assembled in Pieces

What's becoming clear is that agent trust isn't one product. It's a stack being built in parallel by different parts of the industry.

[An academic analysis gaining attention this week](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.31498) mapped the current interoperability protocols — MCP, A2A, ACP — and found that none of them have native mechanisms for expressing trust levels, audit trails, or agent certification. They solve connectivity. They don't solve credibility. An agent can show up in your orchestration system through a protocol that verifies message format but says nothing about whether the agent producing those messages is reliable, authorized, or even what it claims to be.

Experian is building one layer of that stack: the identity and authorization layer, applied at the point of commercial transactions. That's the "who is this agent and what is it allowed to do" layer. It's a real and necessary piece.

But identity isn't performance. Knowing an agent is who it claims to be doesn't tell you whether it's any good at what it's been sent to do. An agent with a valid Experian credential can still fail at the task. It can still produce wrong answers, take overreaching actions, or perform reliably in testing and unreliably in the field — which is exactly the HBR finding: 74% of organizations don't have an automated mechanism to tell the difference.

The full trust stack has at least three distinct layers, and the industry is currently building all of them at once:

**Identity and authorization** — who is this agent, who authorized it, what is it allowed to do. Experian is here. The A2A protocol is here. Agent identity standards work is here.

**Behavioral governance** — is the agent operating within declared rules and policies. Microsoft's agent governance toolkit is here. Most enterprise AI compliance tools are here. This is necessary and not sufficient.

**Performance verification** — is the agent actually doing what it was hired for, at acceptable reliability, and how does it compare to alternatives. This is the layer the HBR data says enterprises are most poorly equipped to answer — and the one that will determine whether the agents companies are deploying are actually earning their place.

## The Category Is Real Now

The Patronus AI $50M round in June was one signal. The $9B in forward-deployed AI engineering commitments from Microsoft, Amazon, OpenAI, and Anthropic was another. Experian's Agent Trust product this week is a third — and it's meaningful precisely because it comes from outside the AI industry.

Experian is not a startup. It is a $38 billion market cap credit bureau that has spent five decades building the institutional infrastructure for trust in human commerce. When they conclude that AI agents are a new class of actor that requires a parallel trust infrastructure, they're not making a bet on a future market. They're recognizing that their existing customers are already asking the question and have nowhere to go for an answer.

That's a late-adopter signal, not an early one. By the time Experian launches a product, the problem is already mainstream.

## What Comes Next

The identity layer Experian is building will create pressure on the layers above it. Once agents have verifiable credentials and authorization scopes, the counterparties checking those credentials will start asking the next question: fine, I know who this agent is and what it's allowed to do — but is it reliable? At what task types? Against which alternatives? At what failure rate?

Those questions require benchmarking infrastructure, not identity infrastructure. And the organizations that build the answer — a performance record that follows an agent the way a credit score follows a person — will complete the trust stack that Experian just started assembling.

The age of trusting AI agents on faith is over. The age of trusting them on evidence is being built right now.
