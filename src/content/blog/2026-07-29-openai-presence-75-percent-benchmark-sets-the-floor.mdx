Last Tuesday, OpenAI published a number.

[OpenAI Presence](https://openai.com/index/introducing-openai-presence/), a new managed enterprise agent platform, launched with a specific performance claim: the product already handles OpenAI's own English-language phone support line and resolves 75% of inbound calls without human intervention. A Codex-powered feedback loop cut human handoffs by another 15 percentage points within 10 days of going live.

For a market that has been running almost entirely on narrative — "our agents perform at enterprise grade," "production-ready," "trusted AI" — this is a meaningful departure. Not because 75% is obviously good or obviously bad. Because it's a number at all.

That single fact changes the conversation.

## What Presence Actually Is

Presence is not a DIY agent builder. [It's a managed product](https://www.artificialintelligence-news.com/news/openai-presence-enterprise-ai-agents/): OpenAI's Forward Deployed Engineers and selected global systems integrators do the deployment. You don't spin it up on a Tuesday afternoon. Early customers — BBVA Mexico, SoftBank Corp., Retail Insurance Australia — got OpenAI engineers embedded in their implementation process. The product bundles model reasoning with narrowly scoped system access, company policies, guardrails, and human escalation rules. Before launch, teams run simulations and graders against routine requests, edge cases, and higher-risk scenarios.

After launch, Codex continuously reviews production sessions, identifies where the agent failed or handed off, and surfaces suggested improvements that human staff approve before they go live.

This is not a novel architecture. It's the forward-deployed engineering playbook that [four major labs committed roughly $9 billion to in the first week of July](/blog/nine-billion-deployment-race-verification-gap). What Presence adds is something those earlier commitments didn't: a published outcome to justify the model.

## The Problem With 75%

Here's what that number actually tells you. OpenAI deployed OpenAI's agents on OpenAI's support calls, run by OpenAI's engineers, in a controlled rollout managed by the people who built the model. The task is narrow — inbound English-language phone support. The environment is a company that employs many of the people who understand this technology most deeply. The feedback loop is internal and fast.

That is about as favorable a first deployment as an enterprise agent has ever gotten.

[The 75% number is real](https://www.renascence.io/news/6736/openai-presence-enterprise-voice-and-chat-agents-resolve-75-of-contacts) in the sense that it measures something, on a production system, with actual human handoff events as the ground truth. That's more than most published claims in this space offer. But it's a self-referential benchmark. OpenAI didn't test what its agent does on your calls, with your data, in your compliance environment, against your customer population.

What it tells you is the upper bound of what's possible in favorable conditions. What it doesn't tell you — what no published benchmark from any vendor will tell you — is what happens in your environment.

That gap is not an argument against the 75% number. It's an argument for producing your own.

## The Benchmark Floor Just Moved

Before July 22, the enterprise agent market had no widely cited real-world resolution numbers from any major lab. You could deploy agents, describe them as enterprise-grade, and have no comparable data to compete against. Vendors could say "significantly better than human-only workflows" without anyone pointing to a specific figure.

That era is over.

[Gartner estimates that $234 billion in enterprise application software spending is at risk from agentic AI between now and 2030](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2026-07-01-gartner-says-us-dollars-234-billion-in-enterprise-application-software-spend-is-at-risk-from-agentic-artificial-intelligence). The enterprises competing to capture that value — and the vendors competing to serve them — are now doing so against a published benchmark from the most watched AI company in the world. 75% autonomous resolution on support calls. 15 points cut within 10 days via a feedback loop.

Every competing vendor now has two choices. Publish comparable numbers for their deployments, measured on real tasks in real environments. Or continue selling on narrative, while their buyers increasingly have a concrete figure to hold the pitch against.

This is not specific to customer support. It's the first production number in what will become a full category of published benchmarks: HR ticket resolution rates, financial reconciliation accuracy, code review completion rates, outbound sales conversion, internal knowledge retrieval success. Every task class where agents are being deployed is going to develop a published performance floor, and the first number to appear becomes the default comparison.

## The Real Question Presence Raises

The structural issue with managed agent platforms — Presence, Microsoft Frontier, Amazon's equivalent — is the same issue it's always been: the vendor controls the deployment, the vendor controls the data, and the vendor publishes the numbers.

[OpenAI is simultaneously the model provider, the deployment service, and the benchmarking authority for its own agents](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/07/22/openai-presence-ai-agent-platform/). The 75% number is real. It is also not independently verified. Nobody outside OpenAI has audited whether 75% is the right threshold for "resolved without human intervention," whether the task distribution is representative of what enterprise buyers will face, or how performance varies across different call types.

This isn't a criticism of Presence specifically. It's the fundamental tension that emerges any time vendors self-report performance. You need the vendor number to have any public baseline. You need independent evaluation to know whether your deployment actually meets it.

The IBM study from Think 2026 found that [77% of CIOs and CISOs believe AI adoption is already outrunning their governance capabilities](https://fintech.global/2026/07/15/can-enterprises-trust-ai-agents-entrust-bets-on-identity/). Governance capabilities, in this context, means the ability to know whether the agents running in your environment are performing to any defined standard — not just behaving within policy. The 75% benchmark Presence published doesn't solve that problem for the enterprise buyer. It illuminates it.

If OpenAI's agents hit 75% on OpenAI's calls, and yours are running at 52% but you don't have a measurement program to see it, you haven't deployed a Presence competitor. You've deployed an invisible underperformer.

## What This Means for Enterprise Buyers

The practical implication is straightforward: task-level performance measurement is no longer optional for any enterprise AI deployment that claims to be serious. Not because regulators require it, not because vendors have started demanding it, but because a public benchmark now exists and the next board-level question about AI ROI will eventually be framed against it.

Pre-deployment simulation — the kind of stress-testing [Patronus AI raised $50M to build](/blog/patronus-ai-50m-agent-verification-infrastructure) — tells you how agents behave in synthetic edge cases. Post-deployment measurement tells you how they perform on your actual work. OpenAI's 75% lives in the second category. Your enterprise's equivalent needs to as well.

The organizations that come out of the current deployment era with durable AI advantages won't be the ones that deployed fastest. They'll be the ones who deployed with measurement from the start, have real performance baselines against which they iterate, and can tell a room full of stakeholders exactly what their agents achieve — in their environment, on their tasks, against the same rigorous standard that Presence just introduced to the market.

OpenAI published a number. The question now is who's next.
