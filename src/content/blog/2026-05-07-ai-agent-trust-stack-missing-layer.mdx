Yesterday, [Collibra launched its AI Command Center](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/collibra-launches-ai-command-center-to-scale-agentic-ai-with-real-time-oversight-and-continuous-control-302763105.html) — a product the company describes as a solution to the "hallucination tax." CEO Felix Van de Maele's phrase for the hidden cost of manual oversight, rework, and risk that accumulates as enterprises scale AI agents in production. The Command Center gives organizations a unified view of every agent in their stack, traces decisions in real time, and surfaces drift before it becomes an incident.

That launched seven days after two more "trust" products hit the market. On April 30, [DigiCert unveiled an AI Trust Architecture](https://siliconangle.com/2026/04/30/digicert-debuts-ai-trust-framework-secure-agents-models-content/) that gives AI agents cryptographic identities — the same PKI infrastructure that secures websites and code signing, now extended to autonomous systems. The same day, [Experian launched Agent Trust](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260430719198/en/Experian-Announces-Agent-Trust-to-Power-Trusted-AI-Driven-Commerce), a "Know Your Agent" framework that creates a verifiable link between a consumer, their authorized devices, and the AI agents acting on their behalf — integrated with Visa's Trusted Agent Protocol and enforced at the edge by Cloudflare.

Three major companies. One week. Three different architectures for the same category: trust infrastructure for AI agents.

When a data governance company, a certificate authority, and a consumer credit bureau all ship "trust" products for agents in the same seven-day window, something structural has shifted.

## Three Layers, One Week

The products each address a different part of the stack, and each is genuinely useful.

DigiCert's AI Trust Architecture focuses on identity. It extends PKI to agents themselves: an authenticated, signed system whose behavior is tied to a cryptographic record. The company groups the rollout into AI Agent Trust (identity and lifecycle management), AI Model Trust (supply chain verification for the underlying models), and Content Trust (C2PA-based signing for synthetic content). Each layer closes a real gap that was previously addressed informally if at all.

Experian's Agent Trust frames the problem from the commerce side. When an AI agent places an order or authorizes a payment, the merchant on the receiving end has no reliable way to confirm a real, verified person originally approved that action. Agent Trust creates Human-to-Agent Binding — a persistent, verifiable link between a verified identity, a device, and the specific agents that identity has authorized. The fraud and misrepresentation risk is real; the [agentic commerce infrastructure to address it is only now being assembled](https://www.experian.com/blogs/news/2026/04/30/experian-agent-trust/).

Collibra's AI Command Center addresses what happens once agents are running. The company's survey with Harris Poll found that [91% of enterprise technology decision-makers are already developing or deploying agentic AI](https://siliconangle.com/2026/05/06/collibras-new-ai-command-center-promises-cut-back-agentic-hallucinations/), and fewer than half — 48% — have established governance policies to oversee them. The Command Center is a real-time control plane: a unified view of what's deployed, what decisions are being made, and where systems are drifting before an exposure becomes an incident.

Monitoring, identity, authorization. Each leg of a workable trust infrastructure now has a serious product.

## What They All Share

The three products solve distinct problems and were built by companies with different starting points. But they share one architectural assumption: the agent is already deployed.

DigiCert issues certificates to agents that are already live. Experian binds identity to agents that consumers have already authorized. Collibra monitors agents that are already in production. The entire trust stack activates after the deployment decision has been made.

That's not a design flaw — it's a reflection of where the market is. Enterprises are running agents at scale right now, and the infrastructure to manage them lagged the adoption curve. Closing that gap matters. But it means the trust stack doesn't address the moment where the most consequential trust decision actually happens: before deployment, when someone decides which agent gets used for a given task.

## The Hallucination Tax Starts Earlier

Van de Maele's framing is useful and accurate as far as it goes. Every enterprise scaling AI is paying a cost for agent outputs that are wrong, drifted, or inconsistent: manual review, rework, compliance exposure, and the compounding effect of bad outputs that downstream systems have already acted on before a human catches them.

But the tax doesn't start at monitoring. It starts at selection.

The moment you deploy an agent for a task without understanding its actual failure rate on that task type, you've already committed to a hallucination cost you haven't measured. Real-time monitoring tells you when an agent is drifting from expected behavior. It doesn't tell you what expected behavior should look like — or whether the baseline you're monitoring against is good enough for the work you've assigned it.

The 91% / 48% gap in Collibra's survey captures this precisely. 91% of enterprises deploying agents, fewer than half with governance policies. But there's a third number the survey doesn't ask: how many enterprises verified their agents' performance on a representative task distribution before deploying them? Based on [HiddenLayer's 2026 AI Threat Landscape Report](https://adversa.ai/blog/top-agentic-ai-security-resources-may-2026/) — which found that autonomous agents now account for 1 in 8 reported AI breaches, and only 14.4% of AI agents go live with full security and IT approval — that number is substantially smaller than the governance gap suggests.

The failed pilots and incident reports aren't primarily governance failures. They're evaluation failures. Teams selected agents based on vendor benchmarks and demo performance, deployed without characterizing failure modes, and encountered the gap between benchmark conditions and production conditions under the worst possible circumstances.

## Identity Without Reliability

You can give an agent a cryptographic certificate and it still gets the task wrong 40% of the time. You can bind it to a verified human identity and it still halluccinates domain-specific outputs under distribution shift. You can monitor its decisions in real time and still not know, at selection time, whether a different agent would have handled the job better.

Authentication tells you an agent is who it says it is. Authorization tells you a human approved its actions. Monitoring tells you what it's doing right now. None of those questions are the same as: is this agent actually reliable for this specific task, against the data it will actually encounter?

That question requires performance data. Not vendor benchmarks — those are optimized to show capability ceiling, not operational reliability. It requires domain-specific evaluation against the actual task distribution you care about, run adversarially, with results you own and can compare across alternatives. That's how you know what a good monitoring baseline looks like. It's also how you know which agents are worth governing at all.

## What the Stack Is Missing

The trust infrastructure being built this week is necessary. Certificate authorities issuing agent identities, identity providers binding agents to verified humans, data governance platforms monitoring production behavior in real time — these are the right things to build, and the companies building them are serious.

But the foundation underneath all of them is still missing from most enterprise deployments: a clear, independently verified picture of agent reliability before the governance machinery kicks in.

The trust stack now has three layers. The zeroth layer — pre-deployment performance verification, competitive benchmarking, adversarial testing against your actual task distribution — is where the hallucination tax originates. It's also where it's cheapest to address.

Monitor drift, authenticate identity, bind authorization. Do all of it. Just make sure you know you've deployed something worth monitoring first.
