[Gartner released a forecast last year](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027) that should be the first line of every AI strategy document in circulation right now: more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027.

Not deprecated. Not paused for review. Killed.

And not for technical reasons. Gartner analyst Anushree Verma named three causes: escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls. None of those are engineering problems. All of them are evaluation problems.

That distinction matters more than the number.

## What the Data Actually Shows

The 40% headline has a quieter statistic behind it. [Roughly 88% of AI proofs-of-concept never reach wide-scale deployment](https://umesh-malik.com/blog/autonomous-ai-agents-production-gap-2026). If you interpret Gartner's cancellation figure as covering only the projects that made it to a committed budget line — not the POCs that died before anyone noticed — the actual failure surface is considerably larger.

The market numbers amplify this. [McKinsey's 2026 data shows 62% of organizations experimenting with AI agents, but only 23% have scaled them](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/agentic-ai-adoption-statistics/). Gartner's own CIO survey shows 17% have deployed agents at all, while more than 60% plan to within 24 months. The adoption curve is steep. The production curve is flat. The gap between them is where almost everything breaks down.

Gartner also forecasts that [40% of enterprise applications will embed task-specific agents by end of 2026](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-08-26-gartner-predicts-40-percent-of-enterprise-apps-will-feature-task-specific-ai-agents-by-2026-up-from-less-than-5-percent-in-2025), up from less than 5% in 2025. That expansion is real. So is the kill rate. Both things are true simultaneously because the gap between "we shipped an agent" and "the business runs on it reliably" is exactly the gap that the 40% never close.

## The Agent Washing Problem

Verma's other finding deserves more attention than it typically gets. Of the thousands of vendors now claiming agentic AI capabilities, [Gartner estimates roughly 130 are genuine](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027). The rest are what the industry has started calling "agent washing" — chatbots, RPA systems, and workflow automation products rebranded with the word "agent" somewhere in the marketing copy.

This matters for project failure rates in a specific way. If you're selecting an agent based on vendor claims and procurement demos rather than verified task performance, you're making a selection decision with almost no reliable signal. The demo works because it was built to work in demo conditions. What the system does in production — on your data distribution, against your edge cases, with real users in the loop — is a different question. In a market this saturated with washing, vendor incentives run exactly opposite to giving you an honest answer.

The cancellation usually doesn't come at launch. It comes six months in, when accumulated cost of edge case failures exceeds accumulated value of nominal successes, and no one has the documentation to explain why.

## Why "Escalating Costs" Is a Measurement Problem in Finance Clothing

Gartner's first cancellation driver sounds like a budget issue. It's a measurement issue wearing a finance hat.

Costs escalate when scope is poorly defined. Scope is poorly defined when the original project didn't specify what "working" looks like at task granularity. And that specification didn't exist because no one ran the evaluation to produce it.

The pattern plays out predictably. An organization runs a promising pilot in a narrow, favorable environment. The pilot "works" — meaning it doesn't break conspicuously in the conditions it was tested under. The project gets resourced for production. In production, the task distribution is broader, edge cases are real, and error rates are measurably higher than the pilot suggested. Fixing the gap costs more than the pilot did. At some point, someone with budget authority cancels the project rather than continue escalating spend against unclear evidence of return.

The same logic explains Gartner's second driver: unclear business value. An agent that completes 78% of critical tasks correctly is not a business value story in most production contexts. It's a reliability problem. And "unclear business value" is usually what unclear reliability looks like when the finance team writes the postmortem.

[Seventy percent of developers report problems integrating AI agents with existing systems](https://agenticaiinstitute.org/agentic-ai-enterprise-adoption-2026-governance-gap/). Most discover fundamental data infrastructure gaps only after launching. The technical complexity is real. But the organizations that surface those gaps early — during structured evaluation rather than in front of users — are the ones that make it to production.

## The Common Thread Among the Survivors

The distinguishing characteristic of the [23% of enterprises that have actually scaled agentic AI](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/agentic-ai-adoption-statistics/) isn't technical sophistication. It's specificity applied before deployment.

The projects that survive defined what success looked like before they built anything. They specified which tasks the agent was responsible for. They measured its accuracy on those tasks against a baseline. They identified which failure modes were acceptable and which weren't. They had a mechanism for catching errors in production — not just in the test environment — so cost escalation and reliability drift were visible before they became cancellation-level events.

That's a materially higher bar than "the demo impressed the steering committee." It's also a different category of work than building the agent itself. The engineering problem and the evaluation problem require different disciplines, different timelines, and different ownership. Organizations that conflate them tend to discover the distinction at the worst possible moment.

## What the 40% Are Building Right Now

The organizations heading for the kill rate aren't building bad agents. Many are building technically impressive ones — capable systems that perform well in demonstrations and controlled pilots.

What they're not doing is measuring them. They don't have task-level performance baselines. They don't have documented accuracy rates on the specific work the agent is supposed to do in production. They selected the vendor based on claims and benchmark scores the vendor produced rather than empirical performance on their actual use case. When things go wrong in production — and they will, because every production environment differs from every pilot environment — they have no reference point for distinguishing a fixable calibration issue from a fundamental capability gap.

That's the pilot trap at the operational level. It's not that the proof of concept fails. It's that the proof of concept succeeds under conditions the production environment doesn't replicate, and no one built the evaluation infrastructure that would have surfaced that gap while the timeline was still long and the pressure was still low.

Gartner's 40% won't be canceled for poor execution. They'll be canceled for poor measurement.

The 60% that survive are the ones that treated verification as infrastructure — something you build before you need it, not something you scramble to produce when the budget review arrives.
