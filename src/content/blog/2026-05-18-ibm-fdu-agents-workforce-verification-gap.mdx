On May 14, [IBM Consulting announced Forward Deployed Units](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-05-14-A-New-Way-to-Make-AI-Actually-Work-in-the-Real-World). An FDU is a small, senior pod — six people — supported by a digital workforce of specialized AI agents that handle coding, evaluation, testing, and documentation under human direction. According to IBM, [a six-person FDU delivers the output of a thirty-person team](https://app.dealroom.co/news/feed/ibm-launches-forward-deployed-units-six-person-ai-teams-that-deliver-the-output-of-30-person-teams) at materially better economics. FDUs are already running at Riyadh Air, Nestlé, Heineken, and Pearson.

This is not a productivity story. This is a structural reorganization story — and it introduces a verification problem that neither IBM's announcement nor the industry's coverage has addressed directly.

## What an FDU Actually Is

The six humans in an FDU are not doing the same work a thirty-person team did. They're doing a different job. Domain specialists rethink processes. Architects connect strategy to execution. Engineers set direction and review outputs. The agent layer — running on [IBM Consulting Advantage](https://pulse2.com/ibm-new-forward-deployed-units-model-aims-to-accelerate-enterprise-ai-deployment/), a platform that provides reusable AI agents and industry accelerators — handles execution: writing code, running evaluations, generating tests, producing documentation.

This is the right architecture for a lot of real enterprise work. Complex technical delivery genuinely benefits from agents that can parallelize execution, maintain context across long tasks, and run consistently without cognitive load. The argument that small senior humans plus capable agents can outperform large junior teams is structurally coherent, not just marketing.

But the 6:30 ratio carries an implicit assumption that almost nobody is saying out loud.

## The Verification Architecture Embedded in a Thirty-Person Team

When a thirty-person software team ships work, peer review happens as a structural property of the team itself. Developers review each other's code. QA engineers test against requirements that documentation writers helped clarify. Someone notices when a test suite is thin, or when documentation doesn't match the implementation, or when a module's architecture doesn't fit the broader system. These aren't formal processes. They're the natural byproduct of multiple humans with different vantage points working on the same codebase.

When you go from thirty to six, the twenty-four departures don't just represent capacity — they represent review coverage. The agents filling that capacity gap are not replacing headcount symmetrically. Agents that handle coding, testing, and documentation do not peer-review each other. An agent that writes code doesn't catch the flaw in the code it just wrote. An agent that generates tests generates tests for the requirements it was given; it doesn't surface the requirements that were wrong. Documentation agents document what they were told to document.

The six remaining humans cannot review everything thirty people used to produce. That's the whole point of the 6:30 ratio. But that means the implicit verification layer that lived in the thirty-person team is now explicit — it has to be engineered, not assumed.

## What the Readiness Data Actually Shows

[Fivetran's 2026 Agentic AI Readiness Index](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260505250301/en/Fivetran-Launches-2026-Agentic-AI-Readiness-Index-Revealing-Gap-Between-Enterprise-Investment-and-Data-Preparedness-for-Agentic-AI), published May 5 and based on a survey of 400 data professionals across the US, UK, EMEA, and APAC, found that only 15% of organizations are fully prepared to support agentic AI in production. 41% are already using agentic AI in production anyway. [85% are running agentic AI on a data foundation that isn't ready](https://www.fivetran.com/blog/85-of-enterprises-are-running-agentic-ai-on-a-data-foundation-that-isnt-ready), with the top blockers being data quality and lineage (42%), regulatory compliance (39%), and security and privacy risk (39%).

The organizations IBM is deploying FDUs at are enterprise customers — large, well-resourced, with established AI programs. They are almost certainly in better shape than the average respondent in Fivetran's survey. But "better than average" and "ready for agents to handle production coding and testing without systematic output verification" are different thresholds.

Nestlé, Heineken, Pearson: these are organizations with decades of accumulated systems, data models that were designed for different workloads, and operational environments that differ substantially from the clean examples agents were trained and evaluated on. The 37% gap between benchmark performance and production performance documented consistently across enterprise AI deployments doesn't close because the agents are inside an IBM delivery pod. It closes because the agents have been evaluated against the specific domain, data distribution, and edge cases they'll encounter in production.

## The Shift That FDUs Actually Represent

The enterprise AI story for the past several years has been AI-as-tool: a human team uses AI to work faster, and the humans remain responsible for the output. Errors get caught by the humans, because humans are primary actors reviewing AI suggestions. The feedback loop is tight. The blast radius of a bad AI output is bounded by the human in the loop.

FDUs change the architecture. Agents are primary actors. Humans provide direction and governance. The feedback loop is different: a coding agent's output becomes a test agent's input, which becomes documentation, which becomes client deliverable. A mistake in the coding agent's output propagates through the chain before a human sees it — if a human sees it at all. Six humans cannot spot-check everything thirty agents produce before it ships.

This is not a problem unique to IBM. It is the structural consequence of any architecture where AI is the workforce rather than a tool the workforce uses. And it means the question that matters for the FDU model — the question that will determine whether it scales beyond IBM's best clients — is not "can agents do this work?" but "how do you know, before you build the pod, that these specific agents are reliable enough to do this specific work in this specific environment?"

## The Prerequisite IBM Didn't Name

An Agent Card tells you what an agent claims to do. IBM Consulting Advantage provides reusable agents and assets. Neither answers the question that matters before you reduce your team from thirty to six and start routing production work through the agent layer: what is this agent's actual error rate on tasks like the ones I'm delegating to it, in an environment with data distributions like mine?

That requires domain-specific evaluation — not the vendor's benchmark, which demonstrates capability on clean inputs, but measurement against the representative task distribution in your actual environment. It requires adversarial testing on the edge cases that cause the most damage when they go wrong. It requires performance history, not capability declarations, because the agent that behaves correctly at deployment may not behave correctly after the underlying model is updated and prompt configurations drift.

IBM's FDU model will likely work very well for the structured, well-defined problem domains where their early clients are applying it. The agents doing coding and testing are presumably IBM's best, evaluated against the specific use cases before deployment. The six-person pod includes people whose job is to catch what the agents miss.

The risk is the version that gets replicated without the same rigor — organizations that see the 6:30 ratio and restructure around it without asking what's holding that ratio up. The headcount goes down. The verification responsibility doesn't. And unlike the thirty-person team, the verification infrastructure in an FDU doesn't emerge from social dynamics. It has to be built deliberately, before the pod starts operating.

That's the difference between AI as a productivity tool and AI as the workforce. In the first model, verification is ambient. In the second model, it's engineered — or it's absent.
