On August 4, [Obsidian Security closed an $85 million Series D at a $1.1 billion valuation](https://www.obsidiansecurity.com/news/unlocking-ai-potential-securely). The round was led by Crescent Cove Advisors, with Greylock Partners and Menlo Ventures participating. Total raised: over $200 million. Customer base: 60 of the Fortune 500.

What Obsidian does is straightforward to describe and non-trivial to build: they govern AI agents at runtime across third-party enterprise applications. Not before deployment. Not in test environments. While agents are running, touching real systems, making real decisions. Their platform maintains a full inventory of every agent, every MCP server, and every LLM active inside connected enterprise systems. It restricts excessive permissions to production data. It blocks unsanctioned tool usage. It halts privilege escalation in real time.

They just extended that capability to [Anthropic's Claude Code and Cowork](https://www.securityweek.com/obsidian-security-raises-85-million-at-1-1-billion-valuation/), letting security teams control what those agents can access — what files they can read, what tools they can invoke, what actions they're allowed to take.

This is legitimate, necessary infrastructure. It's also solving exactly half of the problem.

## What Actually Drove the Round

The timing of the Obsidian raise is revealing. It landed in the same week that EU regulators quietly removed a compliance gun that enterprises thought was pointed at their heads.

The EU AI Act's high-risk system obligations — Articles 9–17 for providers, Article 26 for deployers — were originally scheduled to take effect August 2, 2026. For enterprises deploying AI agents in financial services, healthcare, HR, and critical infrastructure, this was supposed to be the enforcement date. Full data lineage requirements. Mandatory audit trails. Human-in-the-loop checkpoints for high-stakes workflows. Penalties up to 7% of global annual turnover.

Then the [Digital AI Omnibus entered into force on July 27, 2026](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/The-Digital-AI-Omnibus-Proposed-deferral-of-high-risk-AI-obligations-under-the-AI-Act), published in the EU Official Journal on July 24. The main high-risk Annex III deadline moved from August 2, 2026, to December 2, 2027. Fifteen additional months.

What actually kicked in on August 2 were GPAI enforcement powers and [Article 50 transparency obligations](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/eu-ai-act-august-2026-transparency-obligations-agency-checklist) — chatbot disclosure, AI content marking, deepfake labeling. Important, but not the enforcement teeth enterprises were bracing for.

So the compliance deadline softened significantly. And the market just backed a unicorn built around agent governance. Both things happened in the same two weeks.

That's not a contradiction. It's a signal.

## The Regulator Blinked. The Problem Didn't.

The Digital Omnibus delay happened because enterprises lobbied for more time. They weren't ready. [Only 18% of large enterprises maintain a current inventory of what agents they're running](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-05-05-think-2026-ibm-delivers-the-blueprint-for-the-ai-operating-model-as-the-ai-divide-widens). Seventy percent say their governance frameworks are not fit for purpose. Large enterprises are projected to average over 1,600 active AI agents by end of 2026.

The regulator gave them more runway. But the ungoverned agents are already in production. The risk didn't wait for the compliance calendar.

Obsidian's revenue growth — which the company hasn't disclosed in full but which their customer metrics suggest is substantial — reflects exactly this dynamic. The compliance pressure loosened. The operational pressure didn't. Enterprises aren't buying runtime agent governance because of Article 26 enforcement dates. They're buying it because they've discovered that agents are interacting with enterprise data in ways security teams didn't anticipate, using permissions nobody explicitly granted, through MCP connections nobody inventoried.

That's the gap Obsidian fills. And it's a real gap. The round is deserved.

## The Gap the Round Doesn't Fill

Here's what runtime governance does: it controls what agents are allowed to touch. Access to files. Calls to external tools. Elevation of permissions. Ingestion of sensitive data. These are access control problems, and access control is a solved category of infrastructure. Obsidian adapted it to agents.

Here's what runtime governance doesn't do: it says nothing about whether the agent is performing correctly on the tasks it's authorized to do.

An agent with perfectly scoped permissions can still produce wrong answers. It can misdiagnose the intent of a workflow step. It can hallucinate facts while operating entirely within its sanctioned data scope. It can degrade silently after a model update, producing outputs that are worse than three months ago on the same task class, with no access violation anywhere in the audit trail. It can perform well on the use cases the vendor demonstrated and fail on the distribution of tasks it encounters in production.

Obsidian's platform would observe all of that and report nothing wrong. No privilege escalation. No unsanctioned tool usage. No blocked actions. Fully compliant at the access layer. Quietly degraded at the task layer.

This isn't a hypothetical failure mode. [McKinsey's enterprise AI research documented a 37% gap between benchmark performance and production performance](https://agentmarketcap.ai/blog/2026/04/07/mckinsey-ai-trust-2026-agentic-governance-framework) across deployed agents — meaning the typical agent performs materially worse on real production tasks than on the evaluations used to select it. That gap exists independent of whether the agent is operating within sanctioned permissions.

[Enterprise confidence in fully autonomous AI agents dropped from 43% in 2024 to 22% in 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21012), during the same period that governance infrastructure investment accelerated. Governance investment controls what agents can do. It doesn't explain to an enterprise whether the thing the agent is doing is correct.

## Two Forms of Trust Infrastructure

What the $85M Obsidian round makes visible is that the enterprise AI trust layer is being built in pieces, and the market is funding those pieces unevenly.

Access governance — controlling agent permissions, inventorying MCP connections, blocking privilege escalation — is at unicorn scale. The market understood this problem early, it maps clearly to existing security categories, and the regulatory context reinforced demand. Obsidian is not alone in this space. Microsoft's [Agent Control Specification](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/foundry/build-2026-open-trust-stack-ai-agents/) and ASSERT framework, shipped at Build 2026, address the same layer from a different angle.

Performance verification — knowing whether an agent is actually doing its job correctly, at what reliability rate, on the task distribution that matters, compared to alternatives — is funded but underdeveloped. [Patronus AI's $50M Series B](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/25/patronus-ai-lands-50m-to-build-digital-worlds-that-stress-test-ai-agents/) went to pre-deployment simulation: stress-testing agents in synthetic environments before they ship. That's the pre-deployment piece. The post-deployment piece — continuous measurement of actual task performance in production, competitive benchmarking against real workloads, the signal that tells you when an agent has degraded and what you'd replace it with — remains the gap.

These are not competing approaches. They're sequential requirements.

Access governance without performance verification gives you agents that can't misbehave. It doesn't give you agents you can trust to do their jobs. An agent that isn't stealing data while producing wrong answers is still producing wrong answers.

Performance verification without access governance gives you agents whose task accuracy you understand but whose data exposure you don't control. That's an audit nightmare and a security liability.

Both are required. The market has built one to unicorn scale. The other is the next infrastructure problem enterprises can't afford to defer — especially now that the regulator has given them cover to move slowly.

## The Deadline the EU Didn't Give You

The Digital Omnibus bought enterprises 15 months of regulatory breathing room on high-risk AI compliance. What it didn't change is the rate at which agents are being deployed, the rate at which they're encountering production edge cases nobody tested, and the rate at which ungoverned task-level failures accumulate into the trust collapse that ends careers and programs.

Obsidian just raised $85 million because the market concluded that access governance couldn't be skipped. The same conclusion is forming around performance verification — just a cycle behind, and without the regulatory pressure that accelerated the first wave.

The enterprises that get there first aren't waiting for another deadline. They're the ones asking the question Obsidian's platform doesn't answer: not just what can this agent access, but how well does it actually perform?
