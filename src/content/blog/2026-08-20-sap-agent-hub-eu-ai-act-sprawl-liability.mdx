[SAP just launched an AI Agent Hub](https://news.sap.com/2026/08/agent-sprawl-why-ai-governance-is-now-board-level-issue/) — a centralized control plane for managing autonomous AI agents across enterprise systems. The product announcement is framed around governance, oversight, and the growing challenge of "agent sprawl." What the announcement is actually saying, between the lines, is this: most large enterprises are running AI agents they cannot fully account for, and that problem has gotten serious enough to warrant a dedicated product category.

The timing is not coincidental. On August 2, [the EU AI Act's full enforcement regime went live](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-08-03-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-take-effect-2-august-2026). The European Commission now has active enforcement powers over AI systems deployed in the EU — including fines up to €15 million or 3% of global annual revenue, whichever is higher. Autonomous agents deployed in Finance, HR, and Procurement — exactly where SAP's platform operates — fall squarely within scope. The legal era for AI agents has started, and it arrived with real penalties attached.

## The Sprawl Problem, Quantified

[A survey by SAP LeanIX](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/sap-xtra-sap-launches-ai-121416189.html) found that 98% of companies have already deployed AI agents or plan to do so. Less than half have visibility into an inventory of what they're running. Gartner puts the trajectory in sharper relief: the average Fortune 500 enterprise will be running more than 150,000 AI agents by 2028. Only 13% of organizations believe they have the governance infrastructure to manage that volume today.

Those numbers describe a specific kind of organizational failure. Agent sprawl isn't about one team spinning up a chatbot that nobody documented — it's about the compounding effect of every team spinning up agents independently, connecting them to production systems, and leaving them running after the pilot ends. The agents persist. The accountability doesn't.

[SAP calls this a board-level issue](https://community.sap.com/t5/technology-blog-posts-by-sap/ai-agents-are-now-a-board-level-risk-is-your-governance-ready/ba-p/14400326) now, and they're right. When an autonomous agent operating in your procurement workflow makes a bad decision — wrong vendor, wrong quantity, wrong contract term — the board question isn't "which IT team approved this." It's "who owns this agent, what are its performance boundaries, and why didn't we know it was failing before it cost us something." The organizations that can't answer those questions are the ones facing regulatory and financial exposure when enforcement authorities start asking the same questions.

## What the EU AI Act Now Requires

The August 2 enforcement activation covers several obligations relevant to deployed agents. Any AI-powered agent or interactive system deployed in the EU must identify itself as AI — not a human — at the start of any interaction. AI-generated content must carry machine-readable attribution. High-risk AI systems operating in regulated domains require documented risk management, human oversight provisions, and audit trails the regulator can actually inspect.

[The enforcement scope is extraterritorial](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/04/us-companies-face-eu-ai-acts-possible-august-2026-compliance-deadline): if EU residents are the end users, compliance applies regardless of where the deploying company is based. US enterprises that stood up EU-facing agents without disclosure mechanisms are now operating in violation. The AI Office can demand documentation, conduct evaluations, and request model access. An organization that can't inventory its own agents has a hard time producing that documentation on demand.

The practical problem: most enterprises don't have agent identification baked into their deployment infrastructure. Agents were stood up for productivity, not for regulatory disclosure. The Act requires that what got deployed quietly must now be disclosed clearly.

## What SAP's Hub Does — and Doesn't

SAP's AI Agent Hub addresses the inventory and control layer. It's designed to give enterprises a centralized view of what agents they're running, what systems they're connected to, what permissions they hold, and who owns them. That's the right foundation. If you can't enumerate your agents, you can't govern them, disclose them to regulators, or shut one down when it's behaving badly.

But inventory is the floor, not the ceiling.

Knowing what agents you're running is a precondition for governance. It doesn't tell you whether the agents are performing. The gap between those two questions is exactly where enterprise AI trust is breaking down. [International AI Safety Report data](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.21012) shows enterprise confidence in fully autonomous AI agents dropped from 43% in 2024 to 22% in 2025 — during the same period deployment volume was accelerating. Organizations aren't losing confidence because they don't know what agents they have. They're losing confidence because they can't systematically assess whether the agents they have are any good.

An agent that is properly registered in your hub, compliant with EU disclosure requirements, and connected to the right systems can still be quietly wrong most of the time. Governance infrastructure catches the policy violations. It doesn't catch the silent failures — the wrong answer accepted as correct, the recommendation that tracks a stale signal, the task completion rate that looked fine until it didn't.

## The Moment These Problems Collide

This August is the first month where two pressures are hitting simultaneously: a legal obligation to know what agents you're running, and an operational reality that most organizations don't. SAP's Hub launch and the EU Act's enforcement activation are arriving in the same week for the same reason — the sprawl problem has become too consequential to defer.

The organizations that use this moment to build a real agent accountability practice — inventory plus governance plus verified performance baselines — are going to be in a fundamentally different position from the ones that treat compliance as the finish line. Registering your agents satisfies the regulator's first question. Whether those agents are performing is the question you'll be left with after compliance, and it doesn't go away.

[Agent sprawl at enterprise scale](https://nhimg.org/articles/agentic-ai-identity-sprawl-is-outpacing-enterprise-governance-in-2026/) is being treated as an identity and governance problem. It's also a verification problem. You can govern a broken agent into perfect regulatory compliance. It's still broken.

The EU AI Act created the legal pressure to know what you're running. That pressure is real and it will produce real enforcement actions. But the organizations that come out ahead won't just be the ones that can enumerate their agents — they'll be the ones that can demonstrate their agents are performing, compare alternatives, and retire the ones that aren't earning their place.

That's not a compliance program. It's a measurement program. And the two are not the same thing.
