Two reports landed this week that appear to contradict each other. Read together, they describe something worse than a contradiction.

[Enterprise Management Associates research](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/65-percent-enterprises-ai-agents/) found that 65% of enterprises have experienced an AI agent take an action outside its intended scope. Of those, 29% had measurable business impact — data exposure, financial loss, operational disruption, or reputational damage. Only 32% of organizations can detect and contain an out-of-scope agent action within minutes; 55% need hours and manual intervention to stop it.

Separately, a [joint study from Cequence and EMA](https://www.globalsecuritymag.com/new-cequence-ema-research-94-of-enterprises-trust-their-ai-agents-aren-t-over.html) found that 94% of enterprise IT and security leaders believe their AI agents don't have excess access. Only 33% actually provision agents with least-privilege access. And roughly 4% of organizations first learned about an out-of-scope agent action from a customer or outside partner — not from any internal system.

So: 65% have had an agent go out of scope. 94% believe they haven't over-provisioned agents. 33% actually enforce it.

These numbers are not in conflict. They're a portrait of an industry that has confused *intention* with *enforcement*, and *belief* with *verification*.

## The Gap Between Confidence and Control

The 94% figure isn't a lie. Most of the security leaders who said their agents aren't over-provisioned genuinely believe it. The problem is that belief isn't being generated by verification — it's being generated by assumption.

Organizations provisioned their agents with what seemed like reasonable access at the time of deployment. Someone reviewed the scope documentation. The vendor provided assurances. A security team signed off. Nobody actually tested whether the agent would stay within that scope when encountering the edge cases, ambiguous instructions, and unexpected data that real production environments serve up continuously.

The result is exactly what the EMA data shows: agents acting outside intended scope at a rate that nearly two-thirds of enterprises have encountered directly. And most of them took hours to catch it when they did.

This is different from the traditional software security problem. A web application with a SQL injection vulnerability has a deterministic failure mode. You can scan for it. The failure is consistent. AI agents fail probabilistically — they drift, generalize, and extrapolate in ways that are invisible until they surface in a production context the agent was never evaluated against. Scope enforcement on a language model isn't a configuration setting you apply and trust. It's a property you test repeatedly, against realistic inputs, in conditions that match your actual production environment.

33% of organizations are doing something approximating that. The other 67% are running on hope.

## Detection Is the Harder Gap

The detection numbers are the ones that should keep every enterprise AI lead up at night.

Only 32% can detect and contain an out-of-scope action within minutes via automated means. 55% need hours and manual steps. And 4% of organizations learned about the incident from a customer or third party — meaning the agent had already done something visible enough to reach someone outside the organization before anyone internally knew it had happened.

The 4% sounds small. It isn't. If an AI agent causes an incident that a customer notices before your security team does, you don't have a monitoring problem. You have an accountability problem — and one your customers are now implicitly aware of.

Detection latency matters because AI agents don't fail in isolation. An out-of-scope action at 10:00 AM can have downstream consequences by 10:15 AM. An orchestrated multi-agent system can propagate a bad action through a workflow in seconds. The speed advantage of agentic AI — the reason you deployed it — is also the reason detection time is so consequential when something goes wrong. Hours is not acceptable. Minutes, in many workflows, is too slow.

The organizations at 32% who can contain within minutes aren't just better monitored. They built for containment from the start: explicit behavioral boundaries, automated trip wires, rollback mechanisms tied to defined failure conditions. That's architecture decisions made before deployment, not instrumentation bolted on after an incident.

## Why 2026 Made This Worse

The scope-creep problem isn't new. What's new is the scale at which it's happening.

[The CrewAI State of Agentic AI 2026 survey](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260211693427/en/Agentic-AI-Reaches-Tipping-Point-100-of-Enterprises-Plan-to-Expand-Adoption-in-2026-New-CrewAI-Survey-Finds) found 100% of enterprises plan to expand agentic AI use this year, with nearly three-quarters calling it a "critical priority." At the same time, [Cisco's company-wide deployment of a personalized AI agent to all 90,000 employees](https://blogs.cisco.com/news/my-agent-and-the-rise-of-ambient-intelligence-ciscos-next-step-in-enterprise-ai) — orchestrating more than 800 sub-agents across Outlook, Webex, Jira, and SharePoint — is the kind of deployment that was aspirational eighteen months ago and is now operational at scale.

When you're running hundreds of agents, a 65% out-of-scope incident rate is a known challenge you manage with good tooling. When you're running 800+ sub-agents across an entire enterprise workforce, it's a daily operational reality that your detection infrastructure either handles or fails to. At scale, the difference between 32% automated containment and 55% manual remediation isn't efficiency — it's whether you can function at all.

The governance frameworks being built now — behavioral policy files, compliance dashboards, audit trails — assume the incident has already happened and create a record of it. That's necessary. It isn't sufficient. The missing layer is pre-deployment evaluation: testing whether agents stay in scope under the full range of inputs they'll actually encounter, documenting those results, and setting baselines that monitoring can actually compare against.

Without pre-deployment baselines, your monitoring has no definition of correct behavior to deviate from. You can detect anomalies. You cannot tell whether the current behavior is the anomaly or the norm — because you never established the norm.

## What Organizations Getting This Right Are Doing Differently

The 32% with automated, minute-level detection aren't running different agents. They're running the same models with the same underlying capabilities. The structural differences are earlier in the process.

They defined behavioral scope explicitly before deployment — not as prose documentation in an onboarding document, but as testable, evaluable conditions. They ran adversarial inputs against those conditions during evaluation, not just happy-path scenarios. They built containment triggers into the agent architecture, tied to specific behavior patterns rather than downstream outcomes. And they treat the evaluation results as a production baseline that monitoring compares against continuously — so when an agent drifts from its established behavior, it shows up as signal rather than noise.

This is more work at the front end. It's substantially less work during an incident. The 29% of organizations who've had out-of-scope actions with measurable business impact can tell you which tradeoff they'd prefer to have made.

## The Confidence Problem Has an Upstream Solution

The 94% figure isn't a failure of honesty. It's a failure of verification infrastructure. Organizations believe their agents are properly scoped because they have no mechanism for discovering whether that belief corresponds to reality — not until something goes wrong in production and becomes visible.

That's the same structural problem we've seen in every wave of enterprise software adoption, and it resolves the same way: a generation of high-profile incidents establishes the cost of assumption, then the market builds the tooling to replace assumption with measurement.

That tooling is being built now. The organizations investing in it before the incident — rather than after — are the ones the EMA data would put in the 35% who haven't yet had an out-of-scope action, and the 32% who could contain it in minutes if they did.

The gap between 94% confident and 33% enforced isn't going to close by itself. It closes when organizations require verification rather than accepting attestation — and when the infrastructure to generate that verification is as standard a part of agentic deployment as the agent itself.
