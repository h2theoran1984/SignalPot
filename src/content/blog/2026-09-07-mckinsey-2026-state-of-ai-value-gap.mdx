McKinsey's annual [State of AI survey](https://aiagentstore.ai/ai-agent-news/this-week) landed this week with a number the industry will spend the next quarter arguing about: 40% of large enterprises are now scaling AI agents across one or more business functions — up from 27% a year ago.

That's a 48% year-over-year increase. On the surface it reads like a breakout. Agents have crossed from experimentation to operational reality for nearly half of large organizations. The skeptics lost. The deployment era arrived.

Then you read the next number, and the story changes.

Fewer than 10% of those organizations have achieved measurable value at scale.

That isn't buried in a footnote. It's the central tension the report is built around, and it's the one number that should worry everyone who read the 40% figure as good news.

## What Scaling Actually Means Right Now

The gap between "scaling" and "achieving value" is wider than it sounds, because "scaling" in this context means committing to a deployment — not demonstrating a result. Organizations are signing the contracts, building the pipelines, assigning the headcount. Agents are live. The question that McKinsey's survey makes painfully visible is whether live agents are performing.

Most aren't — at least not in ways that show up in the data that organizations measure.

The cited blockers are familiar: poor data quality and governance. Those two categories account for the primary obstacles to value, according to the survey, which means the failure mode isn't models that can't do the work. It's organizations deploying capable agents into environments that can't support them — messy data infrastructure, no defined performance baselines, governance frameworks that describe what agents are allowed to do without measuring whether they're doing it well.

This is a different problem than the one the industry spent 2024 solving. The technical deployment challenge — getting agents integrated, connected to tools, operating in real environments — is more solvable than it was eighteen months ago. Forward-deployed engineering units from the major labs, cloud-native agent orchestration, standardized protocols: [the infrastructure for getting agents live is genuinely mature now](https://blog.mean.ceo/ai-agents-news-september-2026/). The 48% scaling increase is evidence of that.

The value challenge is not getting easier at the same rate. And that's the structural problem the 40%/10% gap represents.

## The Software Disruption Number Nobody Is Talking About

Buried inside the McKinsey findings is a stat with longer-range consequences than the adoption numbers.

32% of surveyed organizations say they skipped buying at least one software product or feature because they built it internally using agentic coding tools.

That number deserves more attention than it's getting. Enterprise software markets are built on a durable assumption: the build-vs-buy calculation has historically favored buying for almost everything that isn't a core differentiator. Off-the-shelf software wins on cost, time to deploy, maintenance burden, and features that internal teams can't justify building from scratch.

Agentic coding tools are eroding that calculus. Internal development velocity is no longer primarily constrained by engineering capacity. When teams can specify what they need and have an agent draft and iterate the implementation, the time-cost of building shifts dramatically. A third of large enterprises are already acting on this — bypassing purchase decisions they would have made a year ago.

This is early. 32% is a leading indicator, not a market-clearing outcome. But the trajectory is clear: the more capable internal agent tooling becomes, the more enterprise software vendors need to justify their value on dimensions other than "we already built this." Integration, reliability, SLA guarantees, proven performance data.

Which is, incidentally, a problem of verification, not just capability.

## The 10% Ceiling

The less than 10% figure — the share achieving measurable value at scale — will get pushed back on as pessimistic. Critics will argue that the measurement frame is too strict, that "measurable value at scale" is harder to demonstrate than to generate, that organizations are capturing value in ways surveys don't surface.

Maybe. But the Gartner prediction from last year now looks prescient: [over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027) due to escalating costs, unclear business value, or inadequate risk controls. Gartner wasn't predicting that agents would fail technically. They were predicting that organizations wouldn't be able to demonstrate they were working — and would cancel rather than continue funding something they couldn't prove.

The McKinsey data is consistent with that trajectory. You scale something you believe in. You cut something you can't justify. The gap between 40% scaling and 10% succeeding is where the canceled projects are being generated.

## What the Measurement Gap Costs

The pattern across the last twelve months of enterprise AI data is remarkably consistent. You can swap the source — McKinsey, Gartner, Sinch, IBM, OutSystems — and the structure of the finding is the same: high deployment activity, low verified output, and the blockers are always measurement and governance, not capability.

That's not an accident. It's a product of how the industry prioritized. The first wave of enterprise AI infrastructure solved for reach: get agents connected, get them integrated, get them in front of users. That work was necessary. It's largely done.

The second wave needs to solve for evidence: define what correct output looks like before deployment, build the baselines that make production monitoring meaningful, establish comparative benchmarks so organizations can distinguish between "this agent is working" and "this agent is running." Right now those two things are frequently confused.

The organizations that cross from the 40% into the sub-10% aren't deploying better agents. They're building the infrastructure to know whether their agents are good enough — and to make that case to the people allocating budgets.

McKinsey's 2026 State of AI is a deployment report. The value gap it documents is an invitation to build the verification layer.
