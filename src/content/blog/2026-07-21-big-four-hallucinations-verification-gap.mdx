Three incidents. Three of the world's most trusted professional services firms. Less than a year.

**July 2025:** A University of Sydney researcher named Chris Rudge flags that a Deloitte Australia report published on the Australian Department of Employment website [contains fabricated references to academic papers that don't exist](https://fortune.com/2025/10/07/deloitte-ai-australia-government-report-hallucinations-technology-290000-refund/), a quote invented and attributed to a Federal Court judgment, and citations pointing to real scholars who never wrote what the report claims they wrote. The $290,000 taxpayer-funded report had to be revised and Deloitte was forced to return a portion of the fee.

**May 2026:** GPTZero publishes an investigation into an EY Canada report on loyalty-program cybersecurity. Of 27 references in the report, [16 are hallucinated](https://www.computing.co.uk/news/2026/ai/ey-cybersecurity-report-withdrawn-ai-hallucinations). One fabricated citation claims a McKinsey report found $200 million in unredeemed loyalty rewards globally — a McKinsey report that doesn't exist. EY removes the study from its website and says it is reviewing how the report was published.

**June 2026:** Weeks later, a Deloitte report for the Canadian government of Newfoundland and Labrador — a multi-million-dollar health plan — [draws scrutiny after false citations are discovered](https://neuralwired.com/2026/07/07/deloitte-ai-hallucination-finra-2026/). FINRA issues a formal warning in July about AI hallucination as a systemic risk in professional services outputs.

This isn't a streak of bad luck. This is a pattern.

## The Companies Selling Governance Are the Ones Getting Caught

The particular sting of these incidents isn't just the embarrassment. It's who they happened to.

Deloitte and EY are in the business of AI governance. They sell transformation engagements, AI risk frameworks, and governance assessments to enterprise clients who are trying to figure out how to deploy AI responsibly. EY separately deployed its [agentic AI framework to 130,000 Assurance professionals](https://www.ey.com/en_us/newsroom/2026/04/ey-launches-enterprise-scale-agentic-ai-to-redefine-the-audit-experience-for-the-ai-era) earlier this year — one of the largest professional-services AI rollouts in history.

These organizations know what good AI governance looks like. They've written the whitepapers. They've staffed the practices. They were not naive about the risks of AI-generated content when these reports shipped.

And yet the same structural gap appeared in all three cases: AI generated the content, a human reviewer signed off, and the fabricated citations made it to publication. Internal controls didn't catch it. External scrutiny did.

## The Missing Step

When you read these incidents as a pattern, the missing step becomes obvious.

Every one of these reports went through a human review process before publication. In none of them did that review include systematic verification of the factual claims the AI generated — checking that cited papers exist, that the numbers attributed to external sources can be traced to those sources, that the quotes attributed to court documents match the actual documents.

That step was skipped. Not because anyone decided to skip it — because it wasn't part of the workflow. The human reviewer read the document, assessed its structure and argument, and approved it for publication. Checking whether the AI hallucinated the sources it cited wasn't a step that existed.

[GPTZero's team of three people found every hallucinated citation in the EY report](https://gptzero.me/investigations/ey). A single academic caught the Deloitte Australia fabrications. These weren't forensic investigations requiring specialized tooling — they were relatively straightforward checks that anyone with access to the cited sources and a willingness to look could have run.

The question isn't why external researchers found these errors. The question is why the internal review process, run by professional services firms with thousands of experienced reviewers, didn't.

The answer: you can't catch a failure mode that isn't in your checklist.

## The Better-Case Scenario Problem

Here's the uncomfortable implication.

These three incidents represent the best-case scenario for detecting AI hallucinations in enterprise outputs. Consulting reports are static documents — PDF files published on websites that persistent researchers can read, cross-reference, and check. When a citation points to a court case, you can pull the case. When a statistic is attributed to McKinsey, you can look for the McKinsey report. The output is legible, preservable, and auditable.

Enterprise AI agents running inside organizations don't produce reports. They produce decisions. They route customer inquiries, approve expense submissions, draft regulatory filings, update inventory systems, and generate summaries that shape how humans make downstream choices. The output isn't a document someone can audit — it's a transient action, a field in a database, an email that went out.

If an AI agent running an internal research workflow hallucinates a citation that makes it into a strategy memo, nobody publishes that memo on a government website. If an agent processing insurance claims assigns an incorrect billing code based on hallucinated policy language, nobody at GPTZero is going to notice. The failure only surfaces when it produces a consequence visible enough to investigate.

The Big 4 got caught because their reports are public. Most enterprise AI output isn't.

## What This Pattern Tells You About Agent Verification

The common thread across Deloitte Australia, EY Canada, and Deloitte Newfoundland isn't that the AI was unusually bad. [Hallucination rates for major frontier models on long-form professional documents are well-documented](https://www.vectara.com/blog/when-ai-assures-without-evidence-lessons-from-deloittes-dollar290k-hallucination) — they're meaningfully higher than most users assume, and they increase with document length, topic specificity, and the density of citations required. These models generate plausible-sounding references by design.

The thread is that the verification step wasn't in the workflow.

Adding it is not technically complicated. GPTZero's investigation — finding 16 hallucinated citations in an EY report — was completed by a small team doing structured reference checking. That's an automatable process. You can build an agent that takes a draft document and checks every external claim against the actual source. You can make citation verification a gate that documents must pass before human review even begins.

What stops organizations from doing this isn't capability. It's the assumption that human review is sufficient — that a senior professional reading the document will notice if something is wrong. That assumption was false before AI. It's more false now, because AI generates plausible error at a scale and fluency that makes implausibility hard to spot on first read.

The Big 4 incidents aren't an indictment of anyone's intelligence or professionalism. They're a demonstration that human review, operating at the speed and volume AI enables, is no longer a sufficient verification layer on its own.

## The Irony Worth Sitting With

If you hired Deloitte or EY to audit your AI governance framework, one of the questions they'd ask is whether you have systematic output verification in your AI workflows — whether there's a defined process for checking that AI-generated content is factually accurate before it enters downstream decisions.

The answer in their own publishing workflow, at least in the cases that made headlines, was no.

That's not a gotcha. It's a signal about where the industry actually is, as opposed to where it says it is. Governance frameworks that exist on paper haven't yet caught up to operational practice — including at the firms that write the frameworks.

[FINRA's July 2026 warning](https://sherwood.news/tech/ai-hallucinations-appear-to-be-creeping-into-consulting-reports/) won't be the last regulatory signal about this. As AI-generated content enters more consequential workflows — legal filings, regulatory submissions, financial disclosures, audit opinions — the cost of missing the verification step will keep going up.

The step isn't hard to add. The pattern suggests organizations keep not adding it until something goes wrong. The Big 4 incidents are the visible end of that pattern. The less visible end is running in every enterprise where AI is generating output that humans treat as authoritative without systematic checks on whether it's true.
