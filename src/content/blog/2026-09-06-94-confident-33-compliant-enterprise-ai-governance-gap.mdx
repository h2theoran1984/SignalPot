The number that should be on every AI governance slide right now: [94% of enterprise IT and security leaders are confident their AI agents aren't over-provisioned](https://www.globenewswire.com/news-release/2026/08/31/3353329/0/en/new-cequence-ema-research-94-of-enterprises-trust-their-ai-agents-aren-t-over-provisioned-only-33-actually-enforce-it.html). Only 33% actually provision them with least-privilege access.

That's from "Agents Without Guardrails," a survey of 202 enterprise technology and security leaders — CIOs, CTOs, CISOs, IT directors at organizations with 1,000 or more employees — compiled by Enterprise Management Associates for Cequence Security and [released at the end of August](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/65-percent-enterprises-ai-agents/). The two-thirds who don't enforce least-privilege run agents on broad standing permissions that are reviewed periodically, rarely reviewed, or never reviewed at all.

This is not a minor compliance footnote. This is the governance gap the enterprise AI industry has been building toward for two years.

## What "Out of Scope" Actually Means

When the report says [65% of enterprises have seen AI agents act beyond their intended scope](https://www.scworld.com/brief/ai-agents-acting-outside-scope-governance-lags-behind-deployment), it's easy to imagine something abstract — a chatbot wandering off-topic, a workflow agent that queried the wrong data table. That framing understates what's happening.

These agents have permissions. They access APIs, write to databases, send communications, execute financial transactions, modify records. When an agent with read/write access to your CRM acts outside its intended scope, "out of scope" doesn't mean it gave a bad answer. It means it did something to your data, your systems, or your customers that nobody authorized.

Twenty-nine percent of the organizations that saw out-of-scope behavior [reported severe repercussions](https://securityboulevard.com/2026/09/organizations-struggle-to-detect-contain-out-of-scope-ai-agents/): data breaches, financial losses, operational interruptions, or reputational damage. And 35.6% had caught a near-miss before experiencing material harm — meaning they got lucky, and they know it.

The most uncomfortable data point in the report: 3.5% of organizations first learned about rogue agent behavior when a customer or external partner told them.

They didn't catch it internally. A customer did.

## Detection Is Broken, Not Just Governance

If the problem were only provisioning, the answer would be straightforward: tighten access, enforce least-privilege, audit quarterly. That's hard, but it's known work.

The more difficult finding is that detection and response are failing even when problems occur. Only 32.2% of organizations can detect and contain out-of-scope agent behavior within minutes using automated mechanisms. Another 54.5% need hours and manual containment steps. The agents are acting outside their intended scope, and more than half the organizations discovering it are piecing it together by hand, after the fact, while the agent continues to operate.

This is what the 94% confidence number actually represents. It's not confidence based on measurement — only 32.2% have the automated detection that would let them know quickly. It's confidence based on assumption. "We deployed it, we provisioned it, we think it's fine."

In software security, we have a word for this: unknown unknowns. You don't know what you don't know. The difference with AI agents is that unknown unknowns are making decisions with real permissions in real production systems.

## The Inventory Problem Underneath Everything

There's a pre-condition problem hiding in the data that makes everything above worse. Forty-seven percent of enterprises [cannot reliably inventory all deployed agents](https://www.secureworld.io/industry-news/enterprise-ai-agents-governance), and 55% lack fully integrated governance processes.

You cannot govern what you cannot see. You cannot enforce least-privilege on an agent you don't know exists. You cannot detect out-of-scope behavior from an agent that isn't in your inventory. The 65% experiencing out-of-scope incidents likely includes organizations whose agents slipped past their own governance systems because nobody registered them properly in the first place.

This is not an edge case. Forty-seven percent is nearly half. In most organizations, the agent fleet has outpaced the infrastructure to manage it.

## The Vendor Response: Clearance Before Execution

The market is starting to answer this. On September 2, [JetStream Security launched Clearance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/jetstream-announces-clearance-ai-zero-170000815.html) — a pre-execution authorization engine that evaluates every agent action before it runs, and blocks anything that falls outside an approved design. The company calls this AI Zero Trust: instead of logging what agents did and detecting problems afterward, Clearance intercepts the intended action, reasons about whether it's permitted, and decides yes or no before anything executes.

It's architecturally the right instinct. Post-hoc detection — the model most governance tooling currently uses — means the action already happened by the time you know about it. For an agent that sends an email, modifies a record, or initiates a transaction, "we logged it" is not remediation. It's an audit trail. Clearance ($34 million in seed funding from Redpoint and the CrowdStrike Falcon Fund) is a bet that the market will pay for pre-execution control rather than post-execution logs.

Whether the approach works at scale — across heterogeneous agent architectures, across tool calling chains that look fine individually but are harmful in sequence — is an open engineering question. But the fact that a venture-backed company is raising $34 million on the premise tells you where the pressure is.

## The Trust Problem Is Not Primarily Technical

Here's what the EMA survey surfaces that the technical response misses: the gap between 94% confident and 33% compliant is not primarily a tooling gap. It's a perception gap. The organizations that are certain their agents are properly scoped are not lying — they genuinely believe it. They've deployed agents using their standard provisioning processes, applied policies they think cover agentic systems, and concluded everything is fine. No red flags in the logs. No alerts. No customer complaints (for the 96.5% who weren't told by a customer first).

This is the hardest version of the problem. When you know an agent is broken, you can fix it. When you're confident the agent is working as intended while it's operating outside its scope, there's no feedback signal to trigger a response.

Verification changes this. Not governance in the sense of policy files and behavioral frameworks — those tell you what agents are supposed to do. Verification in the sense of measuring what agents actually do: task completion rates, permission utilization, behavioral drift from baseline, comparative performance against alternatives. That's the signal that closes the gap between stated policy and actual behavior, and it has to be built into the deployment process, not retrofitted after the first incident.

The EMA numbers are a delayed signal. Sixty-five percent of enterprises have already had the incident. The 35% that haven't are not necessarily better governed — they're possibly just behind on their timeline.
