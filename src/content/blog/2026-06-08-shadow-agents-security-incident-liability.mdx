Okta surveyed 292 executives and 492 knowledge workers across seven countries in March 2026. [The results](https://www.okta.com/newsroom/articles/ai-agents-at-work-2026-agentic-enterprise-security/) are worth sitting with.

**88% of organizations report suspected or confirmed AI agent security incidents.** 80% have experienced unintended agent behavior. 23% have experienced credential exposure. Only 22% treat AI agents as identity-bearing entities that require formal access controls.

This is not a future risk. This is the current state of enterprise AI deployment. The agents are already running. Most of them are ungoverned. A significant majority of organizations have already had something go wrong.

## The Math on the Visibility Gap

To understand why these numbers are this high, you have to understand the distribution of agents inside most enterprises.

[A survey by C1 AI](https://www.c1.ai/news/press-release/future-of-identity-security-2026) found that 95% of organizations now run AI agents that autonomously perform IT or security tasks — a shift from experimentation to production that happened in less than a year. The average enterprise manages 37 deployed agents. More than half of those agents run without any security oversight or logging.

Where do all these agents come from? The data is consistent across multiple surveys: [68% of employees use AI tools without IT approval](https://optro.ai/blog/shadow-ai-stats). The marketing team deploys a content agent. The finance team builds a reconciliation agent on an open-source framework. The sales team adopts a CRM agent from a third vendor. Six months later, no single person in the organization has a complete list.

This is the shadow agent problem — and it mirrors the old shadow IT problem with one critical difference. Shadow SaaS apps were mostly isolated. They consumed data and produced output, but they didn't coordinate autonomously with each other. Shadow agents, in a world where [A2A is now in production at 150+ organizations](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/a2a-protocol-surpasses-150-organizations-lands-in-major-cloud-platforms-and-sees-enterprise-production-use-in-first-year-302737641.html) and integrated into all three major clouds, can discover and delegate to each other. An ungoverned agent deployed by a business analyst can receive delegated work from an enterprise-grade orchestrator. The blast radius is different.

Zero CISOs report full visibility into how AI is operating across their organization. [66% report only limited visibility](https://www.kiteworks.com/cybersecurity-risk-management/ai-visibility-governance-problem/). The 12% of organizations not reporting security incidents almost certainly aren't incident-free. They just don't have the visibility to detect what's already happened.

## The Legal Dimension Arriving From Berkeley

An analysis published this month in the [Berkeley Technology Law Journal](https://btlj.org/2026/06/multi-agent-ai-is-outpacing-the-liability-frameworks-built-for-single-agent-systems/) makes a concrete argument: multi-agent AI is outpacing the liability frameworks built for single-agent systems.

The argument isn't complicated, but the implications are. Existing product liability law, negligence doctrine, and contractual frameworks were designed for systems where a harm can be traced — from outcome back through causal chain to an identifiable responsible party. Single-agent AI failures are messy but traceable. You can audit the model version, the prompt, the inputs, and the output.

Multi-agent systems create what the analysis calls a "traceability gap." When agent A delegates to agent B, which calls agent C, which returns data that agent A incorporates into a recommendation — those agent-to-agent interactions are typically opaque, unlogged, and difficult to reconstruct after the fact. The harm happened. The chain of delegation is gone. Nobody designed the system to preserve it, because nobody was thinking about the audit trail when they wired the agents together.

[Gartner projects](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-10-21-gartner-unveils-top-predictions-for-it-organizations-and-users-in-2026-and-beyond) "death by AI" legal claims will exceed 2,000 by end of 2026. Those claims have to land somewhere. When there's no trace of what happened inside the agent pipeline, they tend to land on whoever deployed the system and whoever has the largest balance sheet.

## What Okta's Three Questions Actually Ask

At Showcase 2026 in March, Okta [announced a blueprint](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260316388961/en/Okta-Announces-New-Blueprint-for-the-Secure-Agentic-Enterprise) for the secure agentic enterprise, organized around three questions every organization should be able to answer:

**Where are my agents?** Discovery — identifying all known and unknown agents, including shadow agents deployed without IT approval, and registering them as first-class identities.

**What can they connect to?** Access control — centralizing management of every MCP server, tool, API, and database an agent can interact with, with policy enforced at machine speed.

**What can they do?** Behavioral policy — controlling what actions agents are permitted to take and generating auditable logs when they do.

[Okta for AI Agents](https://www.okta.com/products/govern-ai-agent-identity/) launched April 30 as the implementation, alongside [Cross App Access (XAA)](https://www.okta.com/newsroom/press-releases/showcase-2026/) — a new open protocol standardizing how agents connect to applications across system boundaries.

This is serious infrastructure for a serious problem. Discovery and access governance are exactly where most enterprises are currently failing, and the Okta blueprint addresses both directly. Alongside [Microsoft's ACS and ASSERT frameworks](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/foundry/build-2026-open-trust-stack-ai-agents/) — shipped at Build 2026 last week and covering behavioral compliance at the runtime level — enterprises now have real tools for answering all three questions.

Here's what neither answers.

## The Fourth Question

Knowing where your agents are, what they can connect to, and what they're permitted to do tells you nothing about whether they're doing their job correctly.

An agent can be fully discoverable, registered as an identity, operating within declared access controls, with a clean behavioral audit trail — and still misclassify customer intent 25% of the time. Or generate financial projections that degrade systematically on fiscal year-end data. Or route escalations incorrectly because the underlying model has drifted since the last evaluation was run. ACS verifies the walls the agent stays within. It tells you nothing about what the agent does inside those walls.

The fourth question — *how reliably does this agent perform at the work I'm actually delegating to it, compared to alternatives, under real operating conditions?* — is the question that the [37% gap between benchmark performance and production performance](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/04/13/1135675/want-to-understand-the-current-state-of-ai-check-out-these-charts/) across enterprise deployments reflects. Organizations deploy against vendor benchmarks. They discover the production gap after the fact, when the downstream consequences are already in motion.

The legal stakes make the fourth question more urgent, not less. If you can't reconstruct what happened when a multi-agent system fails — the traceability gap the Berkeley Tech Law Journal describes — the organizations that can demonstrate *prior* performance verification are the ones with a defensible position. Systematic evaluation before deployment, logged performance baselines, ongoing monitoring: these are what "due diligence" looks like when an agent's outputs are consequential. The organizations that deployed on vendor benchmark data and never established an independent performance baseline are the ones who, when something goes wrong, have no evidence they checked.

## The Governance Maturity Curve

The enterprise AI governance stack is being assembled layer by layer, and the sequence is now clear:

**Connectivity:** A2A at 150+ organizations, integrated into all three major clouds. Can agents communicate? Functional.

**Discovery and identity:** Okta for AI Agents, Microsoft Agent 365. Do you know where your agents are and who they are? Infrastructure exists; most enterprises aren't using it yet.

**Behavioral governance:** Microsoft ACS, ASSERT, Windows Secure Runtime. Do your agents operate within declared policy, in contained execution environments? Just shipped.

**Performance verification:** Does this agent actually perform reliably at the work you're giving it, compared to alternatives, under real conditions? Still the unsolved layer — and now the layer with legal consequences attached.

The 80% unintended agent behavior rate from Okta's survey is a governance problem in single-agent contexts. In multi-agent pipelines, where agent-to-agent delegations compound failure modes and obscure the audit trail, it's a liability problem with no obvious defense.

The time to establish a performance baseline is before the incident. Not after the subpoena.
