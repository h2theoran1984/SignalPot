[Salesforce's second Agentic Enterprise Index](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/agentic-enterprise-index-insights-2026/), published August 10, opens with a number that belongs in every board deck this fall: the average enterprise went from five activated AI agents to thirteen between February 2025 and April 2026. Deployment time dropped 53% — to 1.9 days on average. Skills per agent tripled, from two to six. Task volume compounded at 15% monthly throughout the period.

That's not an adoption curve. That's an avalanche.

The other number from the same period: [only 13% of organizations believe they have the right AI agent governance in place](https://news.sap.com/2026/08/agent-sprawl-why-ai-governance-is-now-board-level-issue/).

Both numbers are true at the same time. The enterprise world just added 160% more agents in a little over a year, and one in eight companies thinks it can actually manage what it's running. This week, SAP published a piece titled "AI Agent Sprawl: Why AI Governance Is Now a Board-Level Issue" — not a think piece, but an announcement, because SAP launched a product to address it. The [SAP AI Agent Hub](https://ignitesap.com/sap-ai-agent-hub-and-agent-governance/) is a vendor-agnostic command center designed to inventory and govern AI agents, LLMs, and MCP servers across an enterprise regardless of who built them.

That SAP built this in 2026 is not surprising. That it's positioned as a board-level issue rather than an IT issue is notable.

## What the Deployment Numbers Are Actually Saying

The Salesforce data is real, but it's also Salesforce's own platform data from companies running Agentforce consistently. The sample selects for organizations already committed to the technology. Still, the directional signal is clear: enterprises aren't evaluating AI agents anymore. They're scaling them.

The 1.9-day deployment figure is the most structurally significant. When an agent goes from procurement decision to production in two days, the evaluation process that used to precede deployment — scoping, testing, baseline measurement, integration review — has either been compressed dramatically or is simply not happening. Probably both, depending on the organization.

The 15% monthly task volume growth compounds fast. An agent handling a given workload in January will be handling 1.5x that workload in June, assuming the rate holds. That's a different agent in terms of failure surface, load distribution, and edge case exposure. Organizations calibrating for the January workload are increasingly flying blind by summer.

[SAP's own research from LeanIX](https://news.sap.com/2026/08/agent-sprawl-why-ai-governance-is-now-board-level-issue/) puts the scope of the problem directly: 98% of companies have deployed or plan to deploy AI agents, and [50% of those agents operate in silos](https://ienable.ai/blog/agent-sprawl-enterprise-ai-governance-crisis.html) — no shared state, no coordinated governance, no centralized inventory of what's running. By 2028, analysts project the average Fortune 500 enterprise will operate over 150,000 agents. The gap between that number and the 13% governance readiness figure isn't closing on its own.

## The Regulatory Accelerant Nobody Is Ignoring

On August 2, the EU AI Act's high-risk provisions became enforceable — risk management requirements, human oversight mandates, and conformity assessments for AI systems operating in domains like HR, finance, and procurement. These are precisely the functions where enterprise agent deployment is growing fastest. The timing is not coincidental: SAP's agent hub announcement came eight days after enforcement began.

For regulated industries, the governance question just became a compliance question. "We're aware of the sprawl" is no longer a sufficient answer when regulators can ask for audit trails, risk classifications, and oversight documentation for every agent touching a covered workflow. [The EU AI Act's provisions](https://www.ampcome.com/post/ai-agent-governance-framework) apply to AI systems regardless of who built them — which means every ungoverned agent in a regulated pipeline is now a liability that didn't exist before August 2.

SAP's hub automates agent discovery through integrations with SAP, Google, and Microsoft repositories, captures risk ratings per agent, and tracks the lifecycle from proposed to decommissioned. For an enterprise trying to get ahead of an audit, that's exactly the tooling gap being filled.

## The Distinction the Industry Keeps Blurring

Governance and verification are not the same thing. The conflation is causing real problems.

Governance tells you what agents you have, whether they're within declared policy, and whether someone is accountable for each one. SAP's hub addresses governance. It can answer: "Is this agent authorized? Is it operating within its defined scope? Do we have a record that it exists?" Those are necessary questions with significant compliance value.

Verification tells you whether agents are performing — not whether they're authorized to run, but whether they're doing the work correctly. Task completion rates. Accuracy on the actual production workload. Failure mode distribution. How the agent compares to the alternative you didn't choose. Those are different questions, and no governance platform answers them.

The [Gartner projection that 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by 2027](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027) isn't a governance failure. It's a verification failure: organizations that can't establish clear business value for agents they've already deployed. Governance infrastructure won't stop those cancellations, because governance doesn't generate performance baselines. It assumes them.

The 1.9-day deployment window creates a specific problem here. In 1.9 days, there is no time to run task-level evaluations against the actual production workload, document accuracy expectations, or define what a failure looks like so monitoring can detect it. Agents are going live with behavioral policy files but without performance baselines. Governance without verification is an audit trail for something you don't know is working.

## What Comes After the Inventory

The arrival of SAP's governance hub, the Salesforce deployment surge, and the EU AI Act enforcement all point at the same structural moment: the enterprise world has accepted that AI agents are real infrastructure, not experiments. The governance layer is arriving. That's progress.

The next layer — verification infrastructure, task-level benchmarking, comparative performance data against real workloads — is being built right now, and it's what governance depends on to be meaningful. You can inventory every agent in your stack. Without performance baselines, you're cataloging things you can't evaluate. The board question isn't just "what agents do we have?" It's "which ones are worth keeping?"

At 15% monthly task growth, that question will get more expensive the longer it waits.
