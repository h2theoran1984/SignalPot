Three days ago, [the European Commission activated enforcement of the EU AI Act's high-risk provisions](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-starts-enforcing-ai-act-rules-and-new-transparency-requirements-2-august). Articles 9 through 17 — covering risk management, data governance, technical documentation, automatic logging, transparency, human oversight, and accuracy requirements — are now binding. So are the transparency rules requiring AI systems to identify themselves.

The word "agent" does not appear anywhere in the legislation.

That doesn't mean enterprise AI agents escaped it.

## What "High-Risk" Actually Covers

The Act classifies AI systems as high-risk based on what they do, not what they're called. [Annex III lists the categories](https://salt.security/eu-ai-act-compliance): systems used in credit scoring, HR filtering and recruitment, healthcare triage, insurance pricing, emergency services dispatch, access to education, critical infrastructure management.

If your AI agent scores loan applications, filters resumes at volume, recommends treatment pathways, adjusts insurance rates, or triages customer service queues — it's high-risk under the Act, regardless of what your vendor calls the product. The naming convention that's proliferated across enterprise software ("AI assistant," "smart workflow," "intelligent automation") carries exactly zero regulatory weight.

And [Article 26 closes the obvious escape route](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/04/16/eu-ai-act-logging-requirements/): enterprises that deploy third-party-built agents cannot transfer the compliance obligation to the vendor. The deployer remains responsible for demonstrating that the system works as intended, that oversight is operational, and that incidents are reported. You can outsource the build. You cannot outsource the accountability.

## The Requirements Read Like a Verification Checklist

The compliance obligations under Articles 9–17 are specific enough to be uncomfortable.

Article 9 requires an active risk management system — not a risk assessment document filed at procurement, but a system that operates continuously as the AI system runs and is updated when the operating environment changes. Article 12 requires automatic logging sufficient to allow post-hoc reconstruction of what the agent did and why. Article 14 requires human oversight with genuine intervention capability — the ability to halt, override, or reverse an action the agent takes, not just receive a notification after the fact. Article 15 extends accuracy requirements not only to the outputs the system generates, but to the actions it takes.

Read those requirements and substitute "enterprise AI agent" wherever you see "AI system." What they describe is not a compliance framework built on top of agent deployment. They describe what agent deployment should have looked like before August 2.

The industry spent two years building agents. It largely deferred building the infrastructure to know whether those agents were actually correct, measurably reliable, and stoppable when they weren't. The EU Act didn't create new engineering obligations. It formalized obligations the field had already learned the hard way — from the [74% rollback rate in enterprise production](https://sinch.com/news/sinch-releases-ai-production-paradox/), from the [half of enterprises whose agents passed internal evaluations and still caused customer-facing failures](https://valueaddvc.com/pulse/enterprise-ai-evaluation-gap-2026), from the organizations that [couldn't diagnose why they were rolling agents back at all](https://www.theregister.com/ai-ml/2026/05/13/ai-customer-service-bots-get-rolled-back-at-74-of-firms/5239800).

## The Logging Gap Is the Trust Gap

[Help Net Security's analysis of the Act's logging requirements](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/08/04/eu-ai-act-enforcement-ai-models/) points to a specific technical gap most enterprises haven't closed: logs that capture model outputs but omit tool arguments, denials, and human reason codes. Under Article 12, that's not compliant logging. But the compliance gap is just a symptom of the operational gap.

If your logs don't capture what the agent was doing at the moment it took an action — which tools it called, what inputs it saw, what it decided not to do — you don't just have a regulatory problem. You have no ability to reconstruct failures, validate behavior against expectations, or demonstrate that the system is doing what you claimed it would do. You have outputs without provenance.

[Over half of organizations still lack systematic inventories of the AI systems they have running](https://labs.cloudsecurityalliance.org/research/csa-research-note-eu-ai-act-high-risk-compliance-deadline-20/). You cannot log what you can't find. You cannot demonstrate oversight over agents you haven't catalogued. The [Agentic AI Institute's data](https://agenticaiinstitute.org/agentic-ai-enterprise-adoption-2026-governance-gap/) puts 72% of enterprises in production with agents, and only 36% with centralized governance over those deployments. That gap — between "it's running" and "we know what it's doing" — is now a compliance gap, not just an operational one.

## The Transparency Layer Everyone Forgot to Build

The transparency requirements that also took effect August 2 are narrower in scope but immediately visible: [AI chatbots must identify themselves as AI, and AI-generated or altered content must carry machine-readable marks](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-08-03-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-take-effect-2-august-2026). For consumer-facing agents this is table stakes. For enterprise deployments that route AI-generated responses through human-branded channels — customer service email replies, personalized outreach, auto-generated documents carrying a person's name — August 2 introduced a labeling requirement that a lot of workflows weren't designed for.

This is the compliance equivalent of the fire code inspector arriving after the building is already occupied. The structure is standing. The exits are where they were designed to be. Whether they're correctly marked and actually functional under load is a different question entirely.

## What Legal Ahead Looks Like

The organizations that built measurement programs — task-level performance baselines, continuous monitoring against documented behavior expectations, logging that captures agent reasoning not just agent outputs — aren't just operationally ahead. They're legally ahead.

Not because they planned for August 2. Most didn't. They're ahead because the work of making agents trustworthy and the work of making them compliant turn out to require the same infrastructure. You cannot prove your agent manages risk without a risk management system that runs in production. You cannot demonstrate human oversight without the tooling to intervene. You cannot satisfy Article 15's accuracy requirements without knowing what accuracy your agent actually achieves on the tasks you've assigned it.

The GPAI fines under [Article 101 reach 3% of global annual turnover or €15 million](https://beam.ai/agentic-insights/eu-ai-act-enforcement-august-2-2026-gpai-fines), whichever is higher. For large enterprises, that is a material number. But the fine is downstream. The upstream problem is that most organizations cannot currently pass an audit of the agents they're already running — not because the agents are bad, but because the measurement infrastructure to demonstrate their behavior was never built.

August 2 didn't change what good agent deployment looks like. It changed who can be fined for not doing it.
