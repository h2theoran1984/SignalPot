[On Monday, IBM and Microsoft announced Imagine Enterprise](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-11-10-ibm-and-microsoft-announce-a-new-ai-agent-platform-designed-for-industry-specific-transformation), a joint platform for building, running, and governing industry-specific AI agents. The platform draws on IBM Consulting's library of more than 6,000 pre-built agents and assets, with initial frameworks targeting financial services, insurance, and retail. Forward-deployed engineers from both companies will support enterprise adoption. It's a substantial commitment — not a press release with a landing page, but a real platform backed by deployment resources from two of the largest enterprise technology vendors in the world.

[Yesterday, Genpact published survey data from more than 2,000 enterprise executives across 17 industries.](https://media.genpact.com/2026-11-12-79-of-Enterprises-Report-AI-Agents-in-Production,-but-Only-15-Have-Fully-Tied-AI-Investment-to-P-L-Outcomes) The headline: 79% of enterprises already have AI agents in production. The number buried under that one: only 15% have fully tied their AI investment to P&L outcomes.

Both things are true at the same time. The market is flooded with available agents. The market cannot tell which ones are working.

## The 79% and the 15%

Genpact's survey describes a deployment funnel worth looking at carefully. Of the 79% running agents in production, only 20% use them broadly and consistently across functions — 32% described production deployments limited to some functions, 27% running in isolated use cases. The long tail of "in production" means: an agent exists, it runs, someone has it configured. The P&L linkage figure — 15% — is the economic test of whether any of this is delivering.

85% of enterprises cannot connect what their agents are doing to business outcomes. And notably, [85% of those same companies plan to increase AI spending](https://www.newsweek.com/genpact-ai-study-autonomy-dividend-sanjeev-vohra-12683873) — even as 51% of AI initiatives from the past 24 months failed to deliver expected value. The deployment budget is growing faster than the evidence that deployment is working.

That dynamic isn't exactly irrational. The competitive pressure to adopt is real, and the reputational cost of visibly falling behind is steep. But it means the industry is spending faster than it's measuring, and faster than it's learning what actually works. Only 9% of enterprises surveyed have completed significant operating model transformation. That's the number that tells you whether agents are changing anything beyond the tool count.

## What 6,000 Agents Does to This Market

The Imagine Enterprise platform is well-timed from a supply side. Financial services, insurance, and retail are exactly the sectors that have committed the most deployment resources and are deepest into the deployment-without-returns gap. [Earlier this month, Capgemini's World Cloud Report found that 59% of financial institutions are running multi-agent AI, with only 5% having institutionalized the capability to operate it at enterprise scale.](/blog/capgemini-financial-multiagent-ai-5-percent-working)

A platform with 6,000 pre-built agents and forward-deployed engineering support looks like an answer to that problem. It is not an answer to that problem.

The gap is not a supply problem. Enterprises already have agents in production — 79% of them, by Genpact's count. The constraint isn't access to more agents. It's the ability to evaluate the ones they're running: whether a given agent performs well enough to extend its scope, trust it with more decisions, build dependent workflows on top of it.

A catalog of 6,000 is a selection problem layered on top of a measurement problem. If you cannot assess the performance of the agents you already have, more options don't bring you closer to the 15%. They move you further away.

## The Autonomy Dividend

Genpact describes the missing piece as the "autonomy dividend" — the financial returns that should appear in business performance as agents operate autonomously in core workflows. It's a useful frame because it focuses on outcome rather than activity. The question isn't whether agents are running. It's whether they're generating returns that show up somewhere real.

The autonomy dividend doesn't appear as a function of deployment volume. It requires knowing which agents are reliable enough to extend autonomy to — which ones have demonstrated consistent performance against specific tasks, under real conditions, against the edge cases that production actually surfaces. That's a different infrastructure question from "do we have an agent for this workflow?"

IBM and Microsoft's governance layer addresses part of this — observability, compliance controls, runtime identity management. Governance tells you what an agent is doing in production. It doesn't tell you, before you deploy, whether the agent will perform well enough to generate the return you're counting on. That evaluation step — the one that runs before you commit to an agent's scope — is not in the catalog.

## The Direction of the Problem

The enterprise AI market has treated deployment as the primary milestone and results as a downstream consequence that follows naturally from scale. Genpact's data, and Capgemini's before it, describe a market where deployment is widespread and results are rare. The IBM/Microsoft platform adds more deployment options.

What the 85% actually needs is a pre-deployment layer: a way to evaluate an agent's real performance before committing to its scope, before building dependent infrastructure, before authorizing it to operate in workflows where failure has financial consequences. The step between "we have access to an agent for this" and "we have evidence this agent performs well enough to trust it here" is the gap the data is measuring.

A bigger catalog makes that harder, not easier. More options without better evaluation is just a larger selection problem with the same measurement gap underneath it. The enterprises that move from 79% deployed to genuinely in the 15% will do it by building the capability to tell the difference between an agent that runs and an agent that works — before the deployment decision, not after the results don't materialize.
