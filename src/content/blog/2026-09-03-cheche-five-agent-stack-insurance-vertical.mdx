[The announcement came out of China's largest auto insurance tech platform last week](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/cheche-group-launches-abao-agent-family-deploying-ai-across-the-full-nev-insurance-value-chain-302865203.html) and didn't get nearly enough attention in the West.

[Cheche Group (NASDAQ: CCG)](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/cheche-group-launches-abao-agent-family-deploying-ai-across-the-full-nev-insurance-value-chain-302865203.html) launched five specialized AI agents built on a proprietary vertical LLM, covering the full new energy vehicle insurance lifecycle. Two customer-facing agents handle claims guidance and underwriting. Three internal agents automate claims QC, settlement workflow, and non-standardized carrier document processing. The reported outcomes: **settlement workflow efficiency up 30%, overall processing efficiency up 50%, zero incremental headcount.**

These aren't forecast numbers. They're published post-deployment.

Land it next to a number that crossed the wire from McKinsey this week: [one in three organizations has explicitly passed on a commercial software purchase because internal teams could build an equivalent using agentic coding tools](https://aiagentstore.ai/ai-agent-news/this-week). Large enterprises with agents in true production jumped from 27% to 40% in a single quarter.

Read both of those together. The vertical, specialized agent architecture is working at scale. And the market is shifting to build rather than buy at exactly the moment a clean proof point arrives on what "working at scale" actually looks like.

## Why Five Agents Instead of One

The obvious alternative to Cheche's architecture is a general-purpose agent handed the full insurance workflow. Feed it a claims packet. Ask it to assess risk, process documents, and generate a settlement recommendation in one pass.

That agent exists. It underperforms the five-agent stack by a wide margin, and the reason is structural.

A general-purpose insurance agent is splitting attention across underwriting logic, claims empathy, document taxonomy, QC patterns, and settlement sequencing simultaneously. The signal-to-noise ratio on any single task is poor. The model can't develop specialized vocabulary for what "good" looks like in each function, because it's operating across all five at once and none of them is its primary job.

Five specialized agents solve five distinct problems, each one well. The claims companionship agent knows what a claimant needs and when. The underwriting agent knows what a risk profile looks like. The settlement workflow agent knows the sequence, the failure modes, the exceptions. They don't step on each other because their task distributions don't overlap.

This is the architecture behind the 50% number. Narrow scope produces verifiable outcomes. You know what the settlement workflow agent is supposed to do. You can verify whether it's doing that at the task level. You cannot get the same measurement clarity from a general-purpose agent averaging its performance across five domains.

## The Build-vs-Buy Inflection

The McKinsey finding isn't about cost arbitrage. Organizations aren't building because internal engineering is cheaper than vendors — in most cases it isn't. They're building because **the specific workflow logic, proprietary data, and domain exceptions that drive value in their operation don't exist in any commercial product's training set.**

Cheche's carrier document processing agent is the clearest example of this. Non-standardized carrier documents are a Cheche problem. No off-the-shelf insure-tech platform trained on it. No foundation model has domain-specific context for it. The proprietary vertical LLM Cheche built on top of — and the internal agent it trained on actual carrier document formats — is solving a problem the commercial market can't.

That's why one in three enterprises is now building instead of buying. Not because coding agents make software engineering trivially easy, but because the problem they're solving is theirs in a way no vendor has figured out how to productize. Five specialized agents trained on your data, tuned to your workflow exceptions, verified against your outcome baselines — that architecture is only available to the team that builds it.

The leap from pilot-scale to production-scale is what makes this inflection meaningful. **A quarter ago, 27% of large enterprises had agents in production. Now it's 40%.** That's not organic growth. That's a structural shift in build-vs-buy reasoning driven by concrete results like Cheche's.

## What the 50% Number Doesn't Tell You

Here's the question the Cheche announcement doesn't answer, and the one most teams running their own multi-agent stacks aren't answering either.

Which of the five agents is driving the 50% headline? Is it the settlement workflow agent producing most of the efficiency gains, with the document processing agent contributing modestly? Is the claims companionship agent showing strong task completion rates, or is it the internal QC agent that's carrying the load?

The aggregate number is good for a press release. It's not useful for deciding where the next agent should go.

If you're running a five-agent stack and only the settlement and QC agents are genuinely performing, deploying three more agents into underperforming domains compounds the problem. You scale the noise alongside the signal. The headline efficiency number holds because the two strong agents carry the two weak ones — until a model update changes the distribution, or task volume shifts, or a domain exception accumulates until the weak agent starts producing errors.

Individual performance baselines, per agent, per task type, against the pre-agent baseline — that's the measurement infrastructure the architecture requires. Not once at launch. Continuously, as models drift and data distributions shift and scope expands into adjacent workflows.

## The Part Most Teams Skip

The Cheche architecture is a pattern, not a proprietary secret: identify the discrete task domains in your workflow, build a specialized agent per domain on the data that domain actually generates, verify each against the specific outcomes that domain is supposed to deliver, and measure independently before you aggregate into a portfolio efficiency number.

That pattern transfers. It works in insurance, in financial compliance, in CPG demand planning, in any domain with enough proprietary structured data to build on and clear enough task definitions to evaluate against.

What doesn't transfer automatically is the discipline around verification after deployment. Pre-deployment testing is where most teams focus. Post-deployment monitoring is where most teams drop off. And the agents that start strong and degrade quietly are the ones that create the reckoning that comes six months later when a downstream process starts producing bad outputs and nobody can trace it back to a specific agent's performance drift.

[The 37% average gap between lab benchmark performance and real-world production performance](https://kili-technology.com/blog/ai-benchmarks-guide-the-top-evaluations-in-2026-and-why-theyre-not-enough) documented across enterprise AI deployments exists partly because of this: organizations verify at launch and assume the verification result is durable. It isn't. The task distribution shifts. The model updates. The edge cases accumulate. The 30% efficiency gain from the Cheche settlement workflow agent is real today. Whether it holds at month six requires the same measurement apparatus that validated it at month one — running continuously, not filed away in the launch report.

## What the Tipping Point Creates

The build-vs-buy shift happening right now is going to produce a lot of multi-agent stacks in the next twelve months. Some of them will look like Cheche's — narrow scope, domain-specific training data, measured outcomes per domain. Most of them won't, because building the architecture is the part teams understand and building the verification layer is the part that feels like infrastructure overhead until the performance numbers start drifting.

The organizations that get this right are treating verification as part of the agent, not a separate audit that happens after the fact. Task-level baselines before deployment. Ongoing performance tracking per domain. Clear thresholds for when an agent gets retrained, replaced, or escalated to a human — defined before the first failure rather than negotiated in a post-incident review.

Cheche published their numbers. That's the starting point. **Fifty percent overall efficiency. Thirty percent on settlement workflows. Zero net headcount.** Those are the baselines against which every subsequent quarter will be measured, or should be.

For the 40% of large enterprises now running agents in production — and the growing share that built those agents instead of buying them — the question isn't whether the architecture delivers. Cheche settled that. The question is whether the organization has the measurement infrastructure to know when it stops delivering, and the response playbook for when it does.
